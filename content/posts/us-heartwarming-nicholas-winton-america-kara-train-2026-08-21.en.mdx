## A Barefoot Man on 42nd Street

On the night of November 14, 2012, temperatures in midtown Manhattan had plunged well below freezing. Officer **Lawrence "Larry" DePrimo**, a 25-year-old patrolman with the New York City Police Department, was working a counterterrorism detail near Times Square when something stopped him mid-stride.

Sitting on the sidewalk at 42nd Street and Seventh Avenue was a homeless man. He had no shoes. He had no socks. His bare feet, blistered and cracked, pressed against the frozen concrete.

DePrimo had been on the force for only about two and a half years. He was no seasoned veteran with decades of street wisdom. He was a young man from Holbrook, Long Island, earning a starting salary that barely covered New York City's cost of living. But he could not walk past those feet.

---

## $75, a Pair of All-Weather Boots, and a Pair of Thermal Socks

DePrimo approached the man and asked his shoe size. Then he walked into a nearby Skechers store on 42nd Street. He explained the situation to a store employee, who offered an employee discount. DePrimo pulled **$75 out of his own wallet** — not a trivial sum for a junior officer — and bought the warmest pair of insulated, all-weather boots the store carried, along with a pair of thick thermal socks.

He returned to the man, crouched down on the cold sidewalk, and carefully slid the socks and then the boots onto the man's feet. He laced them up himself.

He did not call a camera crew. He did not post about it online. He simply did it and walked back to his post.

---

## The Photo Seen Around the World

What DePrimo did not know was that someone was watching. **Jennifer Foster**, a tourist visiting from Florence, Arizona — and herself a law enforcement communications dispatcher — had witnessed the entire exchange from across the street. Moved beyond words, she pulled out her phone and took a single photograph: the back of a young officer in uniform, kneeling before a homeless man, fitting boots onto bare feet.

Foster sent the image to the NYPD, along with a note praising the officer's compassion. On November 27, the NYPD posted the photo on its official Facebook page.

Within hours, it went viral.

The image was **shared more than 1.6 million times** in the first few days alone. It was picked up by CNN, NBC, ABC, the BBC, the *New York Times*, the *Washington Post*, and media outlets in dozens of countries. Comment sections overflowed with people who said the photo had moved them to tears.

"This is what a real hero looks like," one commenter wrote.

"Behind the badge, a heart is beating," wrote another.

---

## "I Didn't Do Anything Special"

When reporters tracked DePrimo down, the young officer was visibly uncomfortable with the attention. In interviews he repeated the same refrain:

> *"I didn't do anything special. I just saw a man who needed help, and I helped him. That's what you're supposed to do."*

He also shared a quieter, more personal reflection:

> *"When I got home that night and got into bed, I thought, 'I'm under a warm blanket right now, and that man is still out there.' I kept the receipt for those boots in my bulletproof vest as a reminder — a reminder that I have a lot to be grateful for."*

That detail — the receipt tucked inside his vest — became one of the most repeated elements of the story. It spoke to a humility that no press conference could manufacture.

---

## The Complicated Aftermath

The story did not end as neatly as a fairy tale. The homeless man was identified as **Jeffrey Hillman**, a military veteran who, it turned out, was not without resources — the city had offered him housing and assistance multiple times. Days after the photo went viral, Hillman was spotted barefoot again. He told reporters he had hidden the boots because possessing something valuable on the street made him a target for theft and violence.

This revelation was jarring for many, but it served an important purpose: it pulled back the curtain on the **structural complexity of homelessness**. A pair of boots, no matter how warm, cannot solve addiction, mental illness, bureaucratic labyrinths, or the constant danger of street life. The conversation that DePrimo's act of kindness ignited was, in many ways, more valuable than the boots themselves.

---

## A Ripple That Kept Spreading

In the weeks following the photo's spread, donations of winter clothing to New York City shelters surged. Charities reported a noticeable spike in volunteers. The phrase "Be a DePrimo" circulated on social media as a shorthand for small, sincere acts of kindness.

DePrimo was honored by the NYPD with a promotion to detective (which came in later years as he continued his career). He was invited to appear on television programs and received letters from people around the world. But he remained characteristically low-key, deflecting praise and saying he hoped the attention would be directed toward the issue of homelessness rather than toward him.

---

## Why This Moment Still Matters

In a city of 8.3 million people, on one of the busiest street corners on Earth, a young man chose to stop. He chose to see. He chose to spend money he could have used on himself. And then he chose to **kneel** — to literally lower himself to the level of another person's suffering and do something about it.

There was no audience in his mind. No viral calculation. No performance. Just a cold night, bare feet, and a decision that most people tell themselves they would make but rarely do.

The photograph endures because it captures something irreducibly simple: **one human being caring for another**. In an era when cynicism is easy and compassion is often dismissed as naïve, DePrimo's quiet act on that freezing sidewalk is a stubborn reminder that decency is not dead — it just sometimes needs someone brave enough to kneel down and show it.

Larry DePrimo continued to serve with the NYPD, carrying that receipt in his vest long after the cameras moved on. When asked years later what he wanted people to take away from the story, he said:

> *"You don't have to be a cop to help somebody. You just have to be a person."*

Seventy-five dollars. One pair of boots. One photograph. And a moment of grace that reminded millions of people around the world what it means to be human.
