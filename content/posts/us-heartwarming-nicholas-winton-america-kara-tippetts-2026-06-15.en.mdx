## An Ordinary Mother in Colorado Springs

Kara Tippetts (1976–2015) was, by most measures, an ordinary woman. She lived in Colorado Springs with her husband Jason, a church planter, and their four young children: Ella Katherine, Harper Joy, Lake Edward, and James Boy. Her days revolved around packing lunches, delivering soup to sick neighbors, and reading bedtime stories. She found deep meaning in these small acts and gave them a name that would eventually reach millions: **"mundane faithfulness."**

## The Diagnosis That Changed Everything

In the fall of 2012, Kara was diagnosed with breast cancer. She underwent surgery and chemotherapy, and for a brief, hopeful moment, remission seemed possible. But by 2013, the cancer had metastasized to her bones, brain, and liver. She was reclassified as stage IV—terminal. She was thirty-seven. Her oldest child was not yet ten.

That night, she wrote in her journal:

> *"I cannot choose the ending of my story. But I can choose how I write the pages that remain."*

## A Blog That Reached Millions

Kara started a blog called **"Mundane Faithfulness"** to keep friends and family updated on her treatment. What began as a small, private space quickly grew into something far larger. Within months, her posts were being read by hundreds of thousands of people across the country and around the world.

The power of Kara's writing lay in its honesty. She did not present herself as a saint who never flinched at death. She wrote openly about weeping in the shower as her hair fell out, about the searing grief of knowing she would never see her children graduate, about the nights when fear pressed in so heavily she could barely breathe. And yet, at the end of nearly every post, a quiet refrain returned:

> *"Today, I chose to love the person next to me. That is enough."*

Her readers—cancer patients, caregivers, grieving parents, people simply struggling with the weight of ordinary life—found in her words a permission they had not known they needed: permission to be broken and faithful at the same time.

## Letters to Her Four Children

During her final two years, Kara wrote handwritten letters to each of her children. She recorded the songs she loved, the memories of the day she first held each of them, and one unwavering message: *"Even on the days when Mommy isn't here, you are loved."*

In 2014, she published a book titled **"The Hardest Peace: Expecting Grace in the Midst of Life's Hard."** It was not a bestseller by industry standards, but it sold hundreds of thousands of copies through word of mouth alone. For families walking through terminal illness, it became something close to a lifeline.

## The Letter to Brittany Maynard

In late 2014, Brittany Maynard, a twenty-nine-year-old woman with terminal brain cancer, publicly announced her decision to end her life through Oregon's Death with Dignity Act. The announcement ignited a fierce national debate about assisted dying.

Kara, facing the same cliff's edge, wrote Brittany an open letter. She did not condemn. She did not judge. She simply offered another perspective:

> *"Brittany, I won't pretend to know your pain. But I want to tell you—there is beauty even in the last days. It is hard, and frightening, and sometimes ugly. But grace still comes."*

The letter was read more than six million times. CNN, NBC, and dozens of other outlets covered the story. People across America found themselves confronted not with a political argument, but with the quiet witness of two women on the same precipice, choosing differently, each with dignity. The conversation shifted—from *how we die* to *how we live until we die.*

## March 22, 2015

On a Sunday afternoon in early spring, Kara Tippetts died at home in Colorado Springs, surrounded by her husband and children. She was thirty-eight years old.

Hundreds attended her funeral. Many had never met her in person. They had flown in from across the country—strangers bound together by the words of a woman who had, from her bed, reached into their darkest hours and whispered that they were not alone. One woman wrote in the guest book:

> *"Kara helped me survive my last round of chemo. She never knew my name, but she saved my life."*

## What Mundane Faithfulness Left Behind

The blog still exists. Jason raised the four children with the help of their community, and he kept Kara's letters, parceling them out to each child on birthdays and milestones. Ella, now a young adult, has said that her mother's greatest lesson was not a grand one: *"She taught me that faithfulness doesn't have to be extraordinary. It just has to be today."*

Kara Tippetts did not beat cancer. There was no miraculous recovery, no last-minute reprieve. What she did was answer one of life's hardest questions—*How do you live when you know you are dying?*—with the quietest and most powerful answer imaginable.

You love the person next to you. Today. And that is enough.

The **Mundane Faithfulness** blog and Kara's book, *The Hardest Peace*, remain available for those walking through their own difficult seasons. Her story continues to remind readers that courage is not always loud—sometimes it looks like a mother writing a letter, cooking a meal, or reading one more bedtime story, knowing it may be the last.

*"Don't try to change the world. Just love the person next to you today. That is enough."*
*— Kara Tippetts (1976–2015)*
