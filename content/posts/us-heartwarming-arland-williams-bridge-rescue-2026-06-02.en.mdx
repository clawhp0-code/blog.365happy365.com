## A Boy on the River

Chad Pregracke was born in 1975 and raised in East Moline, Illinois, a small town perched on the banks of the Mississippi River. The river was his childhood companion—he swam in it every summer, fished with his father, and explored its islands and backwaters like a frontier scout. To young Chad, the Mississippi was a living, breathing friend.

But as he grew older, something else came into focus. Beneath the sparkling surface lay a graveyard of discarded appliances, barrels, tires, and every conceivable form of human refuse. The Mississippi, the 2,320-mile artery that drains 41 percent of the continental United States, had become a dumping ground. And nobody seemed to care.

## One Man, One Boat

In 1997, at the age of twenty-two, Chad made a decision that would define his life. He wrote letters to every government agency and corporation he could think of, asking for help to clean the river. Almost everyone said no. The idea of a single person tackling the Mississippi's pollution problem seemed absurd.

So Chad did it alone. He scraped together enough money to get a small boat on the water and started hauling trash out of the river with his own hands. In that first year, working solo from sunup to sundown, he pulled out an astonishing 45,000 pounds of debris—refrigerators, washing machines, televisions, and mountains of tires.

"People thought I was crazy," he later recalled. "And honestly, some days I thought they were right. But every refrigerator I pulled out of the water was one less refrigerator poisoning the river."

## Building a Movement

Word spread. A local newspaper ran a story about the young man cleaning the Mississippi by himself. Phone calls started coming in: *"How can I help?"* In 1998, Chad founded **Living Lands & Waters**, a nonprofit organization dedicated to river cleanups and watershed conservation. He converted a barge into a floating headquarters and began organizing community cleanup events along the river.

What started as a handful of curious neighbors became a cascade. Students, retirees, veterans, corporate teams, Scout troops—people from every walk of life showed up on riverbanks with work gloves and determination. Chad's operation expanded from the Mississippi to the Ohio, Missouri, Illinois, and other major waterways, eventually covering **23 rivers** across the American heartland.

Over twenty-five years, more than **120,000 volunteers** have joined Chad's river cleanups. Together, they have removed over **12 million pounds** of trash—roughly 5,400 metric tons—from America's rivers. The haul includes hundreds of refrigerators, car doors, bowling balls, shopping carts, and even a safe that someone once dumped into the current.

## A Million Trees

Chad understood that removing trash was only half the battle. To truly heal the rivers, their banks and floodplains needed restoration. He launched the **MillionTrees Project**, an ambitious initiative to plant one million native trees along the Mississippi River basin. Volunteers planted saplings in degraded wetlands and floodplains, rebuilding habitats for wildlife, reducing soil erosion, and mitigating flood damage. Tens of thousands of trees have already taken root, slowly transforming barren stretches of riverbank into thriving ecosystems.

## CNN Hero of the Year

In 2013, CNN named Chad Pregracke its **Hero of the Year**, recognizing his extraordinary contribution to environmental stewardship. At the awards ceremony, Chad spoke with characteristic humility:

> *"At first, I was just angry about the trash. But the more I cleaned, the more I realized how much the river gives us—clean water, food, transportation, beauty. We were repaying it with garbage. I'm just trying to settle the debt."*

He donated the entire $300,000 prize to Living Lands & Waters.

## The Ripple Effect

Chad's impact extends far beyond tonnage. His work has inspired river cleanup movements in communities across the country. Schools use his story to teach environmental responsibility. State and federal agencies that once ignored his letters now partner with his organization. He has spoken at the United Nations and been recognized by the Jefferson Award for Public Service.

But perhaps the most profound impact is the one that's hardest to measure: the transformation that happens inside the volunteers themselves. Thousands of people have stood knee-deep in river mud, hauled out a rusted appliance, and felt—maybe for the first time—that their individual actions truly matter.

## Still on the River

Today, Chad Pregracke is still on the water. His barges still float from town to town. Volunteers still show up every weekend. And the rivers are cleaner, healthier, and more alive than they were when a twenty-two-year-old launched a battered boat and refused to accept that nothing could be done.

The lesson of Chad Pregracke's life is deceptively simple: **if you see a problem and nobody is fixing it, you can be the one who starts.** One person, one boat, one piece of trash at a time—until 120,000 people and 12 million pounds later, the impossible becomes an accomplished fact.

*"Somebody had to do it. It just happened to be me."*
