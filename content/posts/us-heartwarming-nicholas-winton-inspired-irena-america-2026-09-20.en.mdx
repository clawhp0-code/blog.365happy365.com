# "This Tip Money Belongs to the Kids" — Albert Lexie and 30 Years of Quiet Generosity

## A Shoeshine Box in a Hospital Lobby

In 1981, a man carrying a worn wooden shoeshine box walked into the lobby of Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh in Pennsylvania. His name was **Albert Lexie**, and he was in his early thirties. He had come to do the only thing he knew how to do well — shine shoes.

Albert grew up in **Monessen**, a small steel town about 30 miles south of Pittsburgh. He had an intellectual disability that made formal education difficult. Career options were limited. But at fifteen, his father had taught him a trade: shining shoes. Albert took to it with pride and precision, and it became his life's work.

He charged **$3 to $5 per pair**. On a good day, he might earn a few dozen dollars. Yet twice a week — every Tuesday and Thursday — he woke before dawn, boarded multiple buses, and made the grueling 90-minute commute each way to the hospital.

## Every Tip, Every Time

Albert's work was meticulous. Doctors, nurses, administrators, and visitors became loyal customers. Many were generous with tips, sometimes giving several dollars on top of his modest fee.

What none of them knew at first — and what would eventually stun an entire nation — was that **Albert never kept a single cent of his tips.**

Every tip he received went directly into the hospital's **Free Care Fund**, a charity that covers medical expenses for families who cannot afford treatment for their children.

When asked why, Albert's answer was always the same: **"The kids are sick. They need it more than I do."**

This was a man who earned less than $10,000 a year. He lived in a small, modestly heated home. He didn't own a car. He was, by any financial measure, poor. And yet he gave — consistently, quietly, and without fanfare — for more than three decades.

## $200,000, One Dollar at a Time

Albert's donations didn't come in dramatic lump sums. They arrived in crumpled bills — ones, fives, tens — handed over at the end of each workday. A few dollars here. A few dollars there.

From **1981 to 2013**, those small amounts accumulated into something extraordinary. Over the course of more than **30 years**, Albert Lexie donated **over $200,000** to the Free Care Fund.

To put this in perspective: a man who never earned more than minimum wage gave away a significant portion of his lifetime earnings so that other people's children could receive medical care. The math is almost impossible to reconcile — unless you understand that Albert simply viewed his tips as money that was never his to begin with.

## The World Takes Notice

For years, Albert's generosity went largely unnoticed outside the hospital. He didn't seek attention. He didn't give interviews. He just showed up, shined shoes, collected tips, and handed the money over to the fund.

But the hospital staff knew. They watched him do it year after year, decade after decade. Eventually, the story reached local media, and then national outlets. **CNN, ABC News, and newspapers across the country** reported on the shoeshine man who had quietly become one of the most significant individual donors in the hospital's history.

In **2006**, Children's Hospital honored Albert with a special recognition award. He was visibly uncomfortable with the attention. "I'm just shining shoes," he said.

But the hospital's leadership saw it differently. They called him **"a true hero"** — not for a single dramatic act, but for the extraordinary discipline of doing something small and good, every single day, for thirty years.

## Putting Down the Box

In **2013**, declining health forced Albert to retire from his shoeshine work. After more than three decades of twice-weekly trips to the hospital, the lobby felt emptier without him.

On **October 1, 2018**, Albert Lexie passed away at the age of seventy-two. The hospital released a statement mourning him as one of their most beloved figures:

> "Albert Lexie was not a wealthy man. He was not a celebrity. But he was one of the greatest donors this hospital has ever known. The number of children he helped is beyond counting."

## The Measure of a Gift

Albert Lexie's story challenges a deeply held assumption: that meaningful giving requires meaningful wealth. He proved the opposite. **The value of a gift is not determined by its size, but by the sacrifice behind it.**

A billionaire donating a million dollars may barely feel it. Albert Lexie donating $200,000 felt every dollar — in skipped meals, in cold winters, in buses instead of cars. And he did it anyway, because somewhere in that hospital, a child needed surgery, and a family couldn't pay.

His shoeshine box is gone. His chair in the lobby is empty. But the children who walked out of that hospital — healed, alive, given a second chance — carry something of Albert with them, whether they know it or not.

A few dollars at a time. A lifetime of love.

**"This tip money belongs to the kids."**

---

*Albert Lexie passed away on October 1, 2018. The Free Care Fund at UPMC Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh continues to operate, and donations are still made in Albert's name. His story remains one of the most powerful examples of quiet, sustained generosity in American history.*
