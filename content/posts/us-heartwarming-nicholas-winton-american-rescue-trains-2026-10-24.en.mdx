# "I Will Find a Cure Before My Children Die"

## A Diagnosis Without Hope

In 1998, John Crowley and his wife Aileen were raising three children in New Jersey. Their firstborn, John Jr., was healthy. But their daughter Megan and son Patrick were struggling — failing to meet developmental milestones, their muscles weakening with each passing month.

At fifteen months old, Megan was diagnosed with **infantile Pompe disease**, a rare genetic disorder in which the body lacks the enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA), causing glycogen to accumulate in cells and progressively destroy muscle tissue — including the heart and diaphragm. Patrick received the same diagnosis shortly after.

The prognosis was devastating. Most children with infantile Pompe disease do not survive past their second birthday. There was no treatment, no cure, and no ongoing clinical trial that offered meaningful hope.

The doctor's advice was gentle but final: *"Spend as much time with them as you can."*

John Crowley, a finance and marketing executive with no background in medicine or laboratory science, refused to accept that verdict.

## From Business Executive to Biotech Founder

Crowley threw himself into research. Night after night, he read every published paper on Pompe disease, contacted researchers around the world, and attended medical conferences where he was often the only non-scientist in the room. Through this relentless pursuit, he connected with Dr. William Canfield, a researcher at the University of Oklahoma who was exploring **enzyme replacement therapy (ERT)** — the idea of manufacturing the missing enzyme in a lab and infusing it into patients' bodies.

Canfield's research was promising but starved of funding. The patient population was too small for most pharmaceutical companies to consider it commercially viable.

In 2000, at the age of thirty-two, Crowley made a decision that stunned everyone around him. He quit his stable job at Bristol-Myers Squibb and co-founded **Novazyme Pharmaceuticals** with Dr. Canfield. He poured his savings, retirement funds, and borrowed money into the venture. The company had one mission: develop an enzyme replacement therapy for Pompe disease before his children's hearts gave out.

The clock was already ticking. Megan was on a ventilator. Patrick's heart was dangerously enlarged. Crowley estimated he had, at most, two to three years.

## A Race Against Biology

Building a biotech startup is grueling under any circumstances. Building one to save your own dying children adds a dimension of pressure that is almost unbearable. Crowley spent his days pitching to venture capitalists and his nights monitoring his children's breathing machines.

In 2001, pharmaceutical giant **Genzyme Corporation** acquired Novazyme. For Crowley, it was an agonizing trade-off: he surrendered control of his company but gained access to Genzyme's manufacturing capacity, regulatory expertise, and financial resources — without which the drug could never reach patients in time.

Inside Genzyme, Crowley rose to senior vice president. But his dual role as executive and patient-father created an ethical minefield. When concerns about conflict of interest threatened to exclude Megan and Patrick from clinical trials, Crowley stepped down from his position. He chose his children over his career without hesitation.

## The Breakthrough

In 2003, the enzyme replacement therapy drug — later branded **Myozyme (alglucosidase alfa)** — entered clinical trials, and Megan and Patrick were among the patients who received it.

The results were remarkable. Megan's and Patrick's cardiac function stabilized. The relentless muscle deterioration slowed dramatically. The children who were never supposed to reach kindergarten began attending school — in wheelchairs, yes, and with ventilator support, but *alive*, laughing, learning.

In **April 2006**, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration granted full approval to Myozyme, making it the **first-ever approved treatment for Pompe disease**. What one father's desperation had set in motion became a lifeline for thousands of patients worldwide.

## Life Beyond the Prognosis

Megan and Patrick Crowley defied every medical expectation. Megan enrolled at the **University of Notre Dame**, becoming an advocate for disability rights and rare disease awareness. Patrick pursued his own education and interests. Both continued to require wheelchairs and respiratory support, but they lived full, engaged lives — decades beyond what any doctor had predicted.

John Crowley went on to lead other biotechnology companies focused on rare diseases, including Amicus Therapeutics, where he served as chairman and CEO. He has never stopped working on behalf of patients whom the pharmaceutical industry might otherwise overlook.

The family's story was adapted into the 2010 film **"Extraordinary Measures,"** starring Brendan Fraser as John Crowley and Harrison Ford as a composite character based on the researchers who contributed to the breakthrough.

## A Father's Love as a Force of Science

In interviews, Crowley has consistently deflected the word "hero."

> *"People call me a hero. I'm not a hero. I'm just a dad. Any father would do the same thing. I just refused to give up."*

But what Crowley accomplished was, by any measure, extraordinary. He bridged the gap between a parent's love and the cold mechanics of drug development. He turned grief into venture capital, desperation into clinical data, and a father's promise into an FDA-approved medicine.

John Crowley's story is a reminder that the most powerful force in science is not always intellect or funding. Sometimes, it is simply a parent sitting beside a ventilator at 3 a.m., whispering: *"I will not let you go."*

His two children are the living proof that he kept that promise.
