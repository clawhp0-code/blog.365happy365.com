# Men on Iron

## Lewis Hine and the Workers Who Built the Empire State Building

Spring 1930. 34th Street, Manhattan.

A man with a camera appeared at the construction site. He was nearly sixty years old. He made a request to the project supervisor.

"May I go up?"

They let him. They knew who he was. **Lewis Hine** — the photographer who had exposed child labor and changed the law.

He was there to document something different. The construction of the tallest building in the world. He climbed. And he pressed the shutter.

---

## 410 Days

Construction of the Empire State Building began on March 17, 1930.

The goal was singular: surpass every building on earth. The Chrysler Building had claimed the world record only eleven months earlier. The Empire State Building would take it back — and exceed it by far.

The pace was without precedent.

An average of 4.5 floors per day. 3,400 ironworkers in rotating shifts. Four men per rivet: one to heat it in the furnace, one to toss it, one to catch it with tongs, one to drive it home with a hammer. Sparks flew. Iron rang against iron across the Manhattan sky.

Four hundred and ten days later, a 102-story building stood complete.

## No Safety Nets. No Hesitation.

Lewis Hine's photographs show men with nothing between them and the ground.

Workers perched on steel beams. Hundreds of meters of open air below. Men eating lunch, smoking cigarettes, looking out over the city — no fear visible anywhere.

Most of the ironworkers were Mohawk men from Kahnawake, a community near Montreal, Canada. Since the late 19th century, Mohawk workers had built a reputation as the finest high-steel workers in North America — sought after for every major bridge and skyscraper project.

Hine climbed alongside them. He looked where they looked.

His most famous image from this work is titled *Icarus*. A man sits on a steel beam. Behind him, the New York skyline stretches in every direction. He does not look at the camera. He is simply there — between the sky and the iron.

---

## A Final Masterpiece

Lewis Hine had spent his career using the camera to change the world.

In 1908, his photographs of children working in cotton mills helped produce America's first federal child labor law. After World War I, he documented war refugees across Europe with the Red Cross. Immigrants, laborers, the hidden costs of industrialization — his camera found them all.

In 1930, he turned his lens upward.

This project was different. Hine's earlier work had targeted suffering and exploitation. Now he wanted to capture something else: the dignity of labor. The grandeur of what human hands could build. The men who stood without fear at the edge of the sky.

He produced approximately 1,000 photographs. In 1932, a selection was published as *Men at Work* — a book Hine described as a tribute to the American worker.

---

## Poverty and Forgetting

When the Empire State Building opened in May 1931, America was in the depths of the Great Depression.

The world's tallest building had almost no tenants. New Yorkers called it the "Empty State Building." It would take twenty years to turn a profit.

Lewis Hine descended into poverty alongside his country.

Depression-era commissions dried up. He had saved nothing. He lost his home. In 1940, he applied for a position with the Works Progress Administration — and was rejected. He died later that year, largely forgotten.

But his photographs survived.

*Lunch atop a Skyscraper* became one of the most reproduced photographs of the 20th century. Hine's name was often omitted from the credit — but the image he made became a permanent symbol of the American industrial age.

---

## What the Camera Left Behind

The official death count from Empire State Building construction is five workers.

The actual number was almost certainly higher. Thousands of men worked without safety equipment, without insurance, at heights that would be unthinkable under modern regulations.

Lewis Hine's photographs recorded those men. Not as statistics. Not as casualties. As people.

Men sitting on iron beams above the clouds, looking out over a city they were building with their hands.

The building they raised still stands above Manhattan today.
