# "If You Fire on These People, I Will Fire on You"

## The Helicopter Pilot Who Stood Between His Own Army and Innocent Civilians

---

### A Village Called My Lai

On the morning of March 16, 1968, soldiers of Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry Regiment, 23rd Infantry Division (Americal), entered the small hamlet of Mỹ Lai in Quảng Ngãi Province, South Vietnam. Their mission briefing said they would find Viet Cong guerrillas. Instead, they found farmers, mothers, elderly men, and children just beginning their day.

What followed was one of the darkest chapters in American military history. Over the next four hours, soldiers systematically killed unarmed civilians — shooting them in ditches, burning their homes, and slaughtering livestock. The final death toll would be estimated at **347 to 504 men, women, children, and infants**.

But above the carnage, circling in a small OH-23 Raven reconnaissance helicopter, one 24-year-old warrant officer saw what was happening — and refused to look away.

---

### Hugh Thompson Jr.

**Hugh Clowers Thompson Jr.** was born on April 15, 1943, in Atlanta, Georgia, and raised in Stone Mountain. He was a Navy veteran who re-enlisted in the Army to become a helicopter pilot. By March 1968, he was an experienced aviator on his second month of combat operations, providing aerial reconnaissance and support for ground troops.

That morning, Thompson, along with door gunner **Lawrence Colburn** (18 years old) and crew chief **Glenn Andreotta**, flew low over My Lai to scout for enemy positions. Almost immediately, something felt wrong.

Thompson saw bodies — dozens of them — scattered along trails, in rice paddies, and crumpled near the edges of the village. But these weren't enemy combatants. They were old men, women clutching babies, children. He noticed a wounded woman on the ground and marked her position with smoke so ground troops could help her. Then he circled back and watched a captain walk up to her and shoot her dead.

Thompson was stunned. He radioed ground command. He received vague, dismissive responses.

Then he flew over a drainage ditch filled with bodies — and saw movement. People were still alive in that pile of the dead. He radioed again: "There are civilians in that ditch. Can you help them out?"

The response from the ground: *"Roger. We'll take care of it."*

Moments later, Thompson watched soldiers begin firing into the ditch again.

---

### The Decision

Hugh Thompson made a choice that defied every rule of military hierarchy he had been taught.

He landed his helicopter **between the advancing American soldiers and a group of roughly 11 to 16 Vietnamese civilians** huddled in a bunker. He climbed out, approached the ground troops, and told them he was going to evacuate the civilians.

A lieutenant protested. Thompson looked at him and delivered words that would echo through history:

> *"If you fire on these people, I will fire on you."*

He turned to Colburn and gave the order: train the helicopter's machine guns on the American soldiers. If they shot at the civilians, Colburn was to shoot back.

**An American crew was pointing weapons at American infantry.**

Thompson then radioed two Huey gunships to land and airlift the civilians to safety. Meanwhile, Glenn Andreotta waded into the corpse-filled ditch and pulled out a small child — a boy, still alive, clinging to his dead mother. Andreotta carried the boy to the helicopter.

In total, Thompson's actions directly saved at least **11 to 16 civilians** from the bunker and the child from the ditch. His report to superiors upon returning to base also led to the cessation of the operation, likely saving many more.

---

### 30 Years of Silence and Shame

Thompson filed a detailed report about the massacre with his commanding officers. The Army buried it. Official records described the operation as a successful engagement with 128 enemy combatants killed.

It took investigative journalist **Seymour Hersh** to break the story in November 1969, more than a year and a half later. The revelation shocked the nation and the world. But the aftermath was complicated.

While Lieutenant William Calley was eventually convicted of murdering 22 civilians (and served only three and a half years of house arrest), Hugh Thompson — the man who *stopped* the killing — was treated as a pariah.

Congressman **Mendel Rivers** of South Carolina publicly called Thompson a traitor and suggested he be court-martialed. Thompson received death threats. Dead animals were left on his doorstep. Fellow soldiers shunned him. He struggled with PTSD, alcoholism, and a shattered marriage.

For three decades, the military establishment and much of the American public refused to acknowledge that what Thompson did was heroic. In their eyes, he had violated the sacred code: he had turned on his own.

---

### Recognition at Last

It was not until **1998** — thirty years after the massacre — that the U.S. Army finally awarded Thompson, Colburn, and Andreotta (posthumously; he was killed in combat three weeks after My Lai) the **Soldier's Medal**, the highest award for bravery not involving conflict with an enemy.

At the ceremony, Thompson said quietly:

> *"What we did was nothing special. We just did what was right."*

The Army also formally acknowledged the actions of My Lai as a massacre and used Thompson's story as a case study in military ethics training at West Point and other service academies.

---

### Return to My Lai

In **2001**, Thompson and Colburn traveled back to Vietnam. They visited the village of My Lai, now home to a memorial and museum. There, they met survivors — including people they had personally rescued 33 years earlier.

The child Glenn Andreotta had pulled from the ditch was now a grown man named **Đỗ Hoa**. He embraced Thompson and wept.

One Vietnamese villager told Thompson: **"You showed us that not all Americans were bad."**

Thompson later described the visit as the most important experience of his life, saying it finally brought him a measure of peace.

---

### Legacy

Hugh Thompson Jr. died on January 6, 2006, at the age of 62, from cancer. Larry Colburn survived him until 2016.

Thompson's story is now taught in military ethics courses around the world. It stands as perhaps the purest example of **moral courage** in modern military history — the willingness to stand alone against overwhelming pressure, not for glory, not for medals, but because it was simply the right thing to do.

In a war defined by moral ambiguity, Thompson drew a clear, unwavering line. He chose conscience over conformity, humanity over orders, and in doing so, he saved not only the lives of Vietnamese villagers but also — in some small, essential way — the honor of the uniform he wore.

---

*"Moral courage is the willingness to stand up for what is right — even when you must stand alone."*
