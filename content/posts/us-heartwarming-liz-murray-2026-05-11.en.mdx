# "Homeless to Harvard" — The Incredible True Story of Liz Murray

## A Childhood Without a Safety Net

Elizabeth "Liz" Murray was born on September 23, 1980, in the Bronx, New York City. From her very first breath, the odds were stacked impossibly against her.

Both of her parents — Peter and Jean Murray — were severely addicted to cocaine and heroin. The family survived on welfare checks, but almost every cent went straight to drugs. The refrigerator was perpetually empty. As a small child, Liz and her older sister Lisa resorted to eating ice cubes, toothpaste, and whatever scraps they could find. In winter, when the heat was shut off, the two girls huddled together under thin blankets to survive the cold.

When Liz was nine years old, her mother was diagnosed as HIV-positive, infected through shared needles. The family, already fractured, disintegrated entirely. Her parents separated. Liz was shuffled to her grandfather's home, but that environment proved no safer.

School was a luxury she could rarely afford. She was chronically absent, and when she did attend, classmates avoided her because of her unwashed clothes and the smell of poverty that clung to her. By the time she was a teenager, Liz had essentially stopped going to school altogether.

## The Moment Everything Changed

When Liz was fifteen, her mother Jean died of AIDS-related complications. The family had no money for a funeral. Jean Murray was placed in the cheapest coffin the city could provide and buried on Hart Island — New York City's mass burial ground for the unclaimed and indigent.

Liz watched the truck carrying her mother's coffin drive away. In that moment, something broke inside her — and something else was born.

> *"I looked at my mother's life and I saw where it ended. And I was on the exact same path. I knew that if I didn't change something right then, I would end up the same way."*

She was homeless. She slept on subway trains, riding from one end of the line to the other. She slept on park benches, in stairwells, on friends' floors. But inside, a fire had been lit. She was going back to school.

## Two Years, Four Years of Coursework

At seventeen, Liz enrolled at **Humanities Preparatory Academy**, an alternative high school in New York City. Principal Perry Weinger saw something in the disheveled girl who sat before him during the interview — a ferocious hunger not for food, but for knowledge and a different life. He gave her a chance.

Even as a student, Liz had no stable home. She did her homework in hallways, on staircases, under streetlights. She studied on the subway while other passengers slept. She carried everything she owned in a backpack.

The results were extraordinary. **Liz completed the entire four-year high school curriculum in just two years**, earning nearly straight A's with a GPA of 95 out of 100. Teachers were astounded. Here was a student who had missed years of school, who had no desk, no bedroom, no parent helping with homework — and she was outperforming almost everyone.

## The New York Times Scholarship and Harvard

In 2000, Liz applied for the **New York Times College Scholarship**, a prestigious award given to exceptional New York-area students who have overcome extraordinary hardship. The scholarship covered full tuition for four years at the college of the recipient's choice.

In her application essay, Liz wrote her story with raw honesty — the drugs, the hunger, the homelessness, her mother's death, and the unshakable belief that education could change her destiny.

**She won the scholarship. And in the fall of 2000, Liz Murray enrolled at Harvard University.**

The story captured America's heart. A homeless girl from the Bronx, sleeping on subways, had made it to the Ivy League. It seemed impossible. It was real.

## "I Never Hated My Parents"

Perhaps the most moving aspect of Liz's story is not her academic achievement, but her capacity for compassion. She never blamed her parents. She never let bitterness define her.

> *"My parents loved me. They just loved drugs more. But I never hated my mother. She was sick. Addiction is a disease."*

In interviews, she has consistently expressed gratitude even for the hardest parts of her life:

> *"I learned that it doesn't matter what cards life deals you. What matters is how you play the hand."*

## The Road After Harvard

Liz's path through Harvard was not a straight line. Her father Peter was also HIV-positive, and when his health deteriorated, Liz took time off from school to care for him. He passed away during her college years, and she was by his side until the end.

She graduated from Harvard in **2009** with a degree in psychology. That same period saw her story adapted into the 2003 Lifetime television film **"Homeless to Harvard: The Liz Murray Story,"** directed by Peter Levin and starring Thora Birch. The film brought her story to millions of viewers across the country.

In **2010**, Liz published her memoir, **"Breaking Night: A Memoir of Forgiveness, Survival, and My Journey from Homeless to Harvard."** The book became a *New York Times* bestseller and was translated into multiple languages, inspiring readers around the world.

## A Life Dedicated to Inspiring Others

Today, Liz Murray is a **motivational speaker** who travels the globe sharing her story. She founded **Manifest Living**, a company dedicated to helping people unlock their potential regardless of their circumstances. She speaks at schools, corporations, and community organizations, with a particular focus on reaching young people who feel trapped by their backgrounds.

Her message is always the same:

> *"Rock bottom became the solid foundation on which I rebuilt my life."*

She tells audiences that she is not special, not uniquely gifted, not blessed with some talent others lack. She simply made a choice — a choice to believe that her past did not have to dictate her future.

## Why This Story Matters

In a country where approximately 1.3 million students experience homelessness in any given year, Liz Murray's story is not just an inspirational anecdote. It is a challenge. It asks us: How many Liz Murrays are out there right now, sleeping on subway trains, carrying their lives in backpacks, waiting for someone — or something — to give them a chance?

Liz was lucky in some ways. She found a school that believed in her, a scholarship that opened a door, and within herself, an unbreakable will. But she is the first to say that luck is not enough. Systems must change. People must care.
