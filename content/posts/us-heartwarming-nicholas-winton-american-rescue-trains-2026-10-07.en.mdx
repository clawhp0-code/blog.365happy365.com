# "Every Tip Belongs to the Children"
## The Story of Albert Lexie, the Shoe Shiner Who Donated Over $202,000 to a Children's Hospital

---

## A Humble Beginning

Albert Lexie was born in 1942 in Homestead, Pennsylvania, a small steel town just outside Pittsburgh. Born with an intellectual disability, Albert never had the chance to pursue higher education or a conventional career. At age 15, he picked up a shoe-shine kit and began polishing shoes on the streets of his hometown. He charged just $5 per shine, and that was his life—day after day, year after year.

He never owned a car. He rode the bus everywhere. His annual income hovered around $10,000, which placed him well below the federal poverty line. By any conventional measure, Albert Lexie was one of the people who *needed* help, not someone you would expect to *give* it.

But Albert Lexie had a heart that no measure of poverty could diminish.

---

## The Decision That Changed Everything

In 1981, Albert began offering his shoe-shine services at UPMC Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh. Every Tuesday and Thursday, he would ride the bus to the hospital, set up his kit in the corridors, and polish the shoes of doctors, nurses, administrators, and staff.

From the very first day, he made a decision that would define the rest of his life: **every single cent he received in tips would go directly to the hospital's Free Care Fund**—a program that covers medical costs for families who cannot afford treatment for their sick children.

When people asked him why, his answer was always the same:

> *"The kids are sick. I can help. That's all there is to it."*

The tips were small—a dollar here, two dollars there, occasionally five. But Albert never wavered. Not once in over three decades did he keep a tip for himself.

---

## Thirty Years, $202,000

One dollar at a time, the donations accumulated into something extraordinary. By the time Albert retired in 2013 due to declining health, his total contributions to the Free Care Fund had surpassed **$202,000**.

To put that in perspective: Albert earned roughly $10,000 a year from his shoe-shine work. The tips he gave away represented a staggering proportion of his potential income. He lived in a modest home, wore simple clothes, and never indulged in luxuries. Every spare cent went to children he would never meet, whose names he would never know.

The hospital estimated that hundreds of children received critical medical care—heart surgeries, burn treatments, cancer therapies, emergency procedures—funded in part by Albert's quiet, relentless generosity.

---

## The World Takes Notice

For years, Albert's story remained an internal legend at Children's Hospital. Nurses whispered about the shoe shiner who gave away his tips. Doctors made a point of getting their shoes polished on Tuesdays and Thursdays, knowing exactly where the money went.

In 2006, the hospital honored Albert with its **UPMC Children's Hospital Humanitarian Award**—a recognition typically reserved for major philanthropists and community leaders. Albert Lexie, the shoe shiner, stood among them as an equal.

The story broke nationally when CNN, CBS, and other major outlets picked it up. In 2013, Albert was nominated as a **CNN Hero**, and messages of admiration poured in from across the country and around the world. The Pittsburgh City Council passed a formal resolution honoring his contributions. The hospital established a fund in his name to ensure his legacy of giving would continue.

---

## A Final Shine

Albert retired from his shoe-shine rounds in 2013 as his health began to fail. On **October 2, 2018**, Albert Lexie passed away at the age of 76.

The hospital released a statement that day:

> *"Albert Lexie was one of the greatest donors in the history of our hospital. His gift was never about the money. It was about the love behind every dollar."*

His funeral was attended by hospital staff, physicians, and families of children whose lives had been saved, in part, by a shoe shiner's tips. Many of them had never met Albert, but they knew his name. They knew what he had done.

---

## The Measure of a Gift

Albert Lexie's life poses a question that lingers long after his story ends:

*What does it truly mean to give?*

In a world that celebrates billionaire philanthropists and headline-grabbing donations, Albert reminds us of a deeper truth. Generosity is not about the size of the check. It is about the size of the sacrifice. A man who lived below the poverty line, who carried an intellectual disability that the world often used to dismiss him, gave away more of himself than most people ever will.

He didn't have wealth. He didn't have fame. He didn't have influence. He had a shoe-shine kit, a bus pass, and a heart that refused to look away from suffering children.

Over 30 years, those small tips—each one a tiny act of love—added up to more than $202,000 and hundreds of children's lives transformed.

Today, a photograph of Albert Lexie hangs in the lobby of UPMC Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh. Beneath it, a simple inscription reads:

> *"The most humble hands do the greatest work."*

His shoe-shine kit has been preserved as a symbol of what one person, no matter how small or overlooked, can accomplish when they decide that someone else's pain matters more than their own comfort.

Albert Lexie never wrote a bestselling book. He never gave a TED talk. He never trended on social media during his lifetime. But he did something far more rare and far more difficult: **he gave everything he had, every day, for 30 years, and never asked for anything in return.**

In the end, the shoes Albert polished were just leather and lace. But the lives he touched—the babies who survived surgery, the toddlers who overcame illness, the teenagers who got a second chance—those are his true legacy.

And they shine brighter than any pair of shoes ever could.
