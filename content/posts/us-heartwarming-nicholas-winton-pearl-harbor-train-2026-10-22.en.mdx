# "I Can't Just Leave Those People Behind"
## Jesse St. Amant and the Cajun Navy: The Ordinary Fisherman Who Saved Over 100 Lives During Hurricane Katrina

## The Day the Levees Broke

On the morning of August 29, 2005, Hurricane Katrina — one of the most devastating natural disasters in American history — made landfall along the Gulf Coast. Within hours, multiple levees protecting New Orleans failed. Eighty percent of the city flooded. In some neighborhoods, water rose to rooftop level in minutes.

Power lines went down. Cell towers failed. Roads vanished beneath brown, debris-choked water. The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and the National Guard would eventually deploy thousands of personnel, but in those first critical hours — and for some areas, the first critical *days* — official rescue was nowhere to be found.

People climbed into attics. When the water followed them, they punched through roofs with bare fists and kitchen knives. Families clung to chimney tops. Elderly residents in wheelchairs sat on porches as the water rose past their waists. Mothers held babies above the surface and screamed for help that didn't come.

But help did come. It just didn't come from the government.

## A Fisherman Named Jesse

Jesse St. Amant was a lifelong Cajun fisherman from St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana — one of the hardest-hit communities. His family had fished those bayous for generations. He knew every canal, every backwater, every neighborhood by heart.

When the storm passed and the flooding began, Jesse evacuated his own family first. Then he hitched his 16-foot flat-bottom boat to his truck and drove toward the water — not away from it.

"I knew there were people in there," he later told reporters. "I know those streets. I know where the old folks live alone. I know where the families with little kids are. I couldn't just leave."

He had no orders. No uniform. No official authority. He had a boat, a small outboard motor, an axe, and a flashlight.

---

## Three Days on the Water

What Jesse found when he motored into the flooded streets of St. Bernard Parish haunted him for years. An elderly couple stood on their roof in the blazing August sun, dehydrated and disoriented. A young mother leaned out of a second-story window, holding her infant above her head. From one house, he heard pounding — a family of four was trapped in an attic as water crept toward the ceiling.

Jesse took the axe and chopped through the roof. The family crawled out, gasping.

His boat was small. He could carry five or six people at a time. So he ferried them to highway overpasses and elevated ground, then turned around and went back. Again. And again. Ten trips a day. Twenty. When fuel ran out, he siphoned gasoline from abandoned cars half-submerged in the flood. When night fell, he navigated by flashlight through pitch-black streets where the only sounds were water lapping against houses and the distant cries of people waiting to be found.

For three consecutive days, Jesse St. Amant did not stop.

**He personally rescued more than 100 people.**

## The Cajun Navy Is Born

Jesse was not alone. Across Louisiana, Mississippi, and East Texas, hundreds of private citizens — fishermen, hunters, recreational boaters, anyone with a vessel and the will to act — loaded their boats and headed for New Orleans. They came in bass boats and jon boats and kayaks. They came with their own fuel, their own food, their own first-aid kits.

The media began calling them the **"Cajun Navy."**

They had no command structure, no coordination with authorities, and in many cases, no permission. Some were turned away at police barricades. They went around them. Officials later admitted that in the chaos of those first 72 hours, the civilian boat rescuers reached thousands of stranded residents that official channels simply could not get to in time.

Congressional reports and subsequent analyses estimated that the Cajun Navy and similar spontaneous civilian rescue efforts saved **approximately 10,000 lives** during Hurricane Katrina — a number that dwarfed many official operations during the same period.

## What Jesse Lost — and What He Never Mentioned

Jesse St. Amant's own home was destroyed by the hurricane. His fishing boat sustained heavy damage. His livelihood was in ruins. Yet in every interview, he deflected questions about his own losses.

"I lost a house," he said. "Those people almost lost their lives. There's no comparison."

He never filed for recognition. He never sought reward. When reporters called him a hero, he shrugged it off.

"I'm just a guy with a boat. And there were people in the water. That's it."

## A Legacy That Endures

The Cajun Navy did not end with Katrina. When catastrophic flooding struck Louisiana again in August 2016, the civilian fleet mobilized within hours, rescuing more than 30,000 people. When Hurricane Harvey buried Houston, Texas, under unprecedented rainfall in August 2017, Cajun Navy volunteers drove hundreds of miles from Louisiana with their boats in tow and pulled thousands from flooded homes.

By then, the movement had formalized. Organizations like the **Cajun Navy Relief** and the **United Cajun Navy** registered as nonprofits, developed dispatch systems using social media and GPS, and trained volunteers in swiftwater rescue techniques. What began as an instinct — neighbors helping neighbors — became an institution.

But the soul of it remains what it always was: ordinary people who refuse to look away.

## The Meaning of the Story

In the mythology of heroism, we often look for extraordinary people — soldiers, astronauts, leaders with titles and training. Jesse St. Amant had none of that. He was a fisherman with calloused hands and a small boat and an intimate knowledge of his community's geography.

What made him extraordinary was not ability. It was *decision*. In the moment when the water rose and the authorities hadn't come and every rational instinct said to protect yourself and your own, Jesse St. Amant decided to go back.

One hundred times, he decided to go back.

He once said: **"I'm just a guy with a boat. And there were people in the water. That's it."**

But "that's it" was one hundred lives. One hundred families who got to continue. One hundred stories that didn't end in that flood.

Sometimes, that's all heroism is. Not a cape. Not a medal. Just a decision — made over and over and over again — not to leave.

---

*The Cajun Navy remains active today, deploying to hurricanes, floods, and disasters across the United States. Most of the fishermen who motored into the flooded streets of New Orleans in August 2005 were never recorded by name. But the people they saved remember. And the tradition they started — ordinary people showing up with whatever they have — endures as one of America's most powerful examples of grassroots courage.*
