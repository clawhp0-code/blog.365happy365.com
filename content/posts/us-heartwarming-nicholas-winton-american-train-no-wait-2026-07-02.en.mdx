# "Every Tip, Every Penny, for the Children" — The Story of Albert Lexie, the Shoeshine Man Who Donated Over $200,000

## A Man, a Brush, and a Bus Ride

Twice a week, before the sun fully rose over the hills of western Pennsylvania, Albert Lexie (1942–2018) packed his shoeshine kit into a worn bag and boarded a bus in Monessen, a small, faded steel town about 35 miles south of Pittsburgh. The ride took over an hour each way. He had no car — never owned one in his life. But the destination never changed: Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh.

There, in the lobby, he would set up his small shoeshine stand. Doctors, nurses, administrators, and visitors would sit in his chair, and Albert would get to work. A shine cost $3 in the early years, later rising to $5. Tips varied — a dollar here, two dollars there, occasionally five.

What Albert did with those tips is what made him extraordinary.

## The Decision That Changed Everything

Albert began shining shoes at Children's Hospital in 1981. Day after day, he watched children — some bald from chemotherapy, others in wheelchairs, many clutching stuffed animals as they were wheeled into surgery. He saw parents weeping in hallways, families crushed under the weight of medical bills they could never hope to pay.

Then he learned about the hospital's **Free Care Fund**, a program that covered treatment costs for families who couldn't afford them. The fund relied entirely on donations.

Albert made a quiet, irreversible decision: **every tip he earned would go to that fund.**

Not half. Not most. Every single cent.

He kept only his base shoeshine fee for living expenses. The tips — his only chance at a slightly more comfortable life — went straight into the donation envelope.

## $202,000 in Dollar Bills

At first, nobody noticed. Albert was just the shoeshine man in the lobby. But over the years, the hospital's development office began tracking his contributions. The numbers were staggering — not because of any single large gift, but because of the relentless, faithful consistency of small ones.

By the mid-1990s, Albert had donated over $50,000. By the early 2000s, he crossed $100,000. And by 2013, his cumulative giving surpassed **$202,000**.

To grasp the magnitude of this, consider Albert's life. He lived in a modest house with no air conditioning. He never married. He had no savings to speak of. His daily tip income averaged $10 to $15. He could have used that money for a car, for home repairs, for retirement savings, for anything that would have eased his own difficult life.

He chose the children instead. Every single time.

## "I'm Not Rich, But I Can Share"

When reporters eventually found Albert and asked why he gave so much, his answers were simple and unadorned.

**"If a kid is sick and the family can't pay, that's just too sad. My tips might pay for one kid's medicine. That's enough for me."**

He never met the children his money helped. The Free Care Fund operated anonymously — donors didn't know recipients, and recipients didn't know donors. Albert was fine with that. He wasn't looking for gratitude. He was looking for a way to matter.

## A City Discovers Its Quiet Hero

In 2006, Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh awarded Albert its **Most Generous Benefactor** award. In a room full of corporate sponsors and philanthropic foundations, a shoeshine man earning less than minimum wage received the highest honor. The audience gave him a standing ovation.

In 2013, CNN featured Albert in a national broadcast, and his story went viral. Donations poured in — not to Albert, but to the Free Care Fund in his name. The hospital established the **Albert Lexie Fund** to continue his legacy.

The Pittsburgh Steelers invited him onto the field at Heinz Field. When his face appeared on the jumbotron and his story was read aloud, over 60,000 fans rose to their feet. Albert, overwhelmed, simply waved and smiled.

But the following Tuesday, he was back on the bus. Back in the lobby. Back with his brush and his polish and his quiet determination.

## The Shoes He Never Filled

On October 4, 2018, Albert Lexie passed away at the age of 76. He left behind no fortune, no estate, no grand monument. What he left was something the world struggles to produce: **proof that generosity has nothing to do with wealth.**

A doctor at Children's Hospital said it best:

> **"Albert shined shoes, but what he really polished was our souls."**

His shoeshine kit — the brushes, the polish, the worn cloth — was preserved by the hospital as a symbol of what one person can do when they decide that someone else's suffering matters more than their own comfort.

## The Measure of a Life

Albert Lexie's story challenges every excuse we make for not giving. Too poor. Too busy. Too small to make a difference. Here was a man who had almost nothing and gave away what little extra he earned — for 32 years, without interruption, without recognition, without asking for anything in return.

He didn't build a foundation. He didn't write a check with many zeroes. He shined shoes and handed over his tips. And in doing so, he helped hundreds of sick children receive the care they needed.

In the end, Albert Lexie was the richest shoeshine man who ever lived — not in dollars, but in the only currency that truly matters: love, freely and completely given.
