# "I Had No Choice — I Had to Save Them"

**The extraordinary true story of Irena Gut Opdyke, who hid 12 Jewish people beneath a Nazi officer's own home**

---

## A Nursing Student Caught in the Storm of War

Irena Gut was born on May 5, 1922, in Kozienice, Poland. She was the eldest of five daughters in a loving Catholic family. At seventeen, she was studying nursing — dreaming of a life spent healing others. Then, in September 1939, both Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union invaded Poland, and Irena's world collapsed overnight.

She was captured by Soviet soldiers, beaten, and left for dead in a forest. After recovering, she found herself in German-occupied territory, forced into labor — first in a munitions factory, then in a German officers' dining hall in Ternopil (in present-day Ukraine).

It was through a window of that dining hall that Irena witnessed a moment that would define the rest of her life: a Nazi soldier ripped an infant from a Jewish mother's arms and shot the baby in midair. The mother was bayoneted when she screamed.

Irena fainted on the spot. When she came to, she made a vow.

*"Whatever I can do to help, I must do."*

---

## From Scraps of Food to a Daring Plan

Irena began small. Working in the officers' kitchen, she smuggled food — potatoes in her skirt pockets, bread tucked beneath her blouse — to the Jews imprisoned in the nearby ghetto. She forged work passes so that Jewish laborers could move between the ghetto and the laundry facility she supervised, and she helped several escape into the surrounding forests.

But in 1943, everything changed. The Germans began liquidating the Ternopil ghetto — systematically murdering thousands. Irena watched as families she had come to know were marched to their deaths.

Around this time, she was assigned as the personal housekeeper to Major Eduard Rügemer, a senior Wehrmacht officer. His spacious villa came with a large cellar beneath the house — and a dangerous idea took root in Irena's mind.

---

## Twelve Lives Beneath the Floorboards

Over a period of weeks, Irena smuggled Jewish men and women into the villa's underground passages and a concealed section of the basement. She brought them food from the major's own kitchen. She carried away waste. She nursed the sick and comforted the terrified.

At its peak, **twelve Jewish people** — including men, women, and a couple expecting a baby — lived hidden beneath the very floor where Major Rügemer entertained fellow Nazi officers. On some evenings, German soldiers drank and laughed in the parlor directly above while twelve human beings held their breath in the darkness below.

The tension was unbearable. A single cough, a single cry, could mean death for everyone — including Irena.

---

## The Terrible Bargain

The secret couldn't last forever. Major Rügemer eventually discovered the hidden Jews. Irena expected a bullet. Instead, the major offered her a deal: he would keep silent about the Jews if Irena became his mistress.

She was twenty-one years old. A devout Catholic. The choice was agonizing — her body and dignity, or twelve lives.

Irena agreed.

Years later, she spoke about this decision with unflinching honesty:

> *"It was not a choice. A choice means you have two options. I had only one — to keep those people alive."*

She carried the shame and pain of that bargain quietly for decades, but she never once regretted it.

---

## Liberation and a New Life in America

In 1944, the Soviet army's advance liberated Ternopil. All twelve of the Jewish people Irena had hidden survived. After the war, Irena spent years in displaced persons camps before immigrating to the United States in 1949. She settled in California and married William Opdyke, a United Nations official, becoming Irena Gut Opdyke.

For nearly thirty years, she said almost nothing about her wartime experiences. She raised her daughter, lived quietly, and tried to heal.

Then, in the late 1970s and 1980s, Holocaust deniers began gaining public platforms. Irena heard people claiming that the Holocaust had never happened — that the camps, the ghettos, the mass graves were fabrications.

She could stay silent no longer.

---

## Breaking the Silence

Irena began speaking publicly — first at local schools, then at universities, churches, synagogues, and national conferences. Her testimony was electric: specific, emotional, unsparing. She described what she had seen, what she had done, and what it had cost her.

In 1999, she published her memoir, *In My Hands: Memories of a Holocaust Rescuer*, co-written with Jennifer Armstrong. The book became a staple in American schools and was widely praised for its unflinching honesty and its message of individual moral responsibility.

Israel's Yad Vashem memorial recognized Irena as one of the **Righteous Among the Nations** — a non-Jew who risked her life to save Jews during the Holocaust. Her story was adapted into the critically acclaimed play *Irena's Vow*, which ran on Broadway in 2009 starring Tovah Feldshuh.

---

## A Legacy That Lives On

Irena Gut Opdyke died on May 17, 2003, at her home in Yorba Linda, California. She was eighty-one years old. The twelve people she saved went on to build families of their own — their descendants now number in the dozens, scattered across Israel, Europe, and the United States.

But Irena's legacy extends far beyond those twelve lives. Through decades of public speaking and her widely read memoir, she planted a single, powerful idea in the minds of hundreds of thousands of students:

> *"You are never too young or too powerless to make a difference. One person can change the world."*

She was not a soldier. She was not wealthy or well-connected. She was a young woman with no weapon but her conscience and no shield but her courage. And beneath the floorboards of a Nazi officer's home, that was enough.

---

## Why This Story Matters Today

In an era when hatred, indifference, and moral cowardice often dominate the headlines, Irena Gut Opdyke's story is a reminder of what ordinary human beings are capable of when they refuse to look away. She did not wait for permission. She did not wait for someone else to act first. She simply decided that the lives hidden beneath her feet were worth more than her own safety — and she acted on that decision every single day for months on end.

Twelve hearts beating in the darkness. One woman's footsteps on the floor above, carrying bread, carrying hope, carrying the quiet, terrifying weight of choosing good in a world gone mad.

That is the story of Irena Gut Opdyke. And it is a story America — her adopted home — should never forget.
