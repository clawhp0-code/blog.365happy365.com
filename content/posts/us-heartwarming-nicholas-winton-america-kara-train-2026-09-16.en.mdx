## A Diagnosis No Parent Should Hear

In March 1998, John F. Crowley and his wife Aileen received the kind of news that reshapes a life in an instant. Their fifteen-month-old daughter, Megan, was diagnosed with **infantile Pompe disease**, a rare genetic disorder in which the body cannot produce enough of an enzyme called acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA). Without it, glycogen accumulates in muscle cells, progressively destroying the heart and skeletal muscles. Most children with the infantile form do not survive past the age of two.

Months later, their newborn son Patrick received the same diagnosis. Their eldest son, John Jr., was unaffected. The Crowley family was suddenly living inside a medical nightmare with no available treatment and a merciless clock.

Doctors were compassionate but blunt: "There is no cure. We can only manage symptoms."

## An Unlikely Entrepreneur

Crowley was not a doctor or a scientist. He was a finance professional working at Bristol-Myers Squibb, with an MBA from Harvard Business School. But when the medical establishment told him nothing could be done, he refused to accept that verdict.

Night after night, he scoured medical journals and databases, teaching himself the biochemistry of lysosomal storage disorders. He discovered that Dr. Yuan-Tsong Chen at Duke University had been researching enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) for Pompe disease in a small laboratory setting. The science was promising but starved of funding. Major pharmaceutical companies saw no profit in a disease affecting only a few thousand people worldwide.

Crowley reached a turning point. If no company would develop the drug, he would build one himself.

In 2000, he resigned from his secure corporate position and co-founded **Novazyme Biopharmaceuticals** in Oklahoma City. He assembled a small team of scientists, pitched to venture capitalists, and poured every ounce of his business acumen into a single mission: creating an enzyme that could keep his children alive.

## The Agonizing Tradeoff

By 2001, Megan was confined to a wheelchair and dependent on a ventilator at night. Patrick's muscle strength was declining rapidly. The window of opportunity was closing.

Crowley made a calculated gamble. He sold Novazyme to **Genzyme Corporation**, one of the world's leading biotech firms specializing in rare diseases, and joined the company as a senior executive to oversee the drug's development from the inside. The acquisition gave the program access to Genzyme's vast clinical infrastructure and manufacturing capabilities — resources Novazyme could never have built alone in time.

But the decision introduced an excruciating ethical dilemma. Under FDA regulations, a high-ranking executive at the company developing a drug faces a **conflict of interest** if his own children are enrolled in the clinical trial. Crowley had to navigate internal ethics reviews, adjust his corporate role, and endure public scrutiny — all while watching his children's health deteriorate.

## The First Infusion

In 2003, Megan and Patrick finally received their first infusions of the experimental enzyme replacement therapy. Within weeks, doctors observed measurable improvement: Megan's dangerously enlarged heart began to shrink, and Patrick's respiratory function stabilized.

It was not a cure. Pompe disease would remain a lifelong condition requiring regular infusions. But for the first time, the disease's relentless progression had been **halted**.

## FDA Approval and a New Era

On April 28, 2006, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved Genzyme's drug **Myozyme (alglucosidase alfa)** — the first-ever approved treatment for Pompe disease. The approval was a landmark not only for the Crowley family but for the entire rare disease community. It demonstrated that even the most obscure conditions could attract serious scientific investment when driven by passionate advocacy.

## Life Beyond the Prognosis

Megan and Patrick Crowley defied every statistical expectation. The children who were told they would not live past two grew up, attended school, made friends, and celebrated milestones their parents had once feared they would never see. Both enrolled at the **University of Notre Dame**, a moment Crowley described as one of the proudest of his life. As of the mid-2020s, both are alive and continuing treatment.

The family's story was adapted into the 2010 film **"Extraordinary Measures,"** starring Brendan Fraser as John Crowley and Harrison Ford as a fictional composite of the researchers involved. While Hollywood took creative liberties, the emotional core remained faithful: a father's love pushing the boundaries of what was thought medically possible.

Crowley went on to hold leadership positions at several biotech and pharmaceutical companies, including Amgen, continuing to champion rare disease research.

## The Lesson

John Crowley had no laboratory training, no medical degree, and no pharmaceutical experience when he started. What he had was something no textbook could teach: the desperate, unshakeable love of a parent who simply could not sit still while his children slipped away.

In an interview, he once said:

> **"Giving up would have been the same as telling my children they weren't worth fighting for. I could never do that."**

Today, thousands of Pompe disease patients around the world receive enzyme replacement therapy that traces its lineage directly back to Crowley's improbable crusade. The drug did not begin in a gleaming corporate research park. It began in a small house in New Jersey, where a father listened to his daughter's labored breathing and decided that failure was not an option.

His story stands as proof of a simple, staggering truth: sometimes the most powerful force in medicine is not a molecule or a machine — it is a parent's refusal to let go.
