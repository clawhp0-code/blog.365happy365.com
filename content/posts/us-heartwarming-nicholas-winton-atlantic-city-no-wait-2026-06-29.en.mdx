# "You Have to Finish" — The Teenage Runner Who Carried a Fallen Rival Across the Finish Line

## A Champion Before the Moment That Made Her Famous

On June 2, 2012, the Ohio High School Athletic Association (OHSAA) State Track and Field Championships were being held at Jesse Owens Memorial Stadium in Columbus, Ohio. Among the competitors was **Meghan Vogel**, a 17-year-old junior from West Liberty-Salem High School — a small school in a small town, but with a runner who had big talent.

That day, Meghan had already won the **1,600-meter race**, claiming the state championship with a commanding performance. She was on top of the world. But with the 3,200-meter event still ahead, her legs were already drained. She had given everything she had.

She lined up for the 3,200-meter race anyway.

---

## The Final Straightaway

The two-mile race took its toll quickly. Meghan, still recovering from her earlier exertion, fell steadily behind the pack. By the final lap, she was near the very back of the field. It wasn't the race she had hoped for, but she kept going.

Then, with roughly 20 meters to go before the finish line, the runner directly in front of her — **Arden McMath**, a competitor from Arlington High School — suddenly collapsed onto the track. Arden's body had given out. Total exhaustion had overtaken her legs, and no matter how hard she tried, she could not stand back up. She crumpled on the hot rubber surface, her race seemingly over.

The crowd gasped.

---

## The Decision That Changed Everything

Meghan Vogel had a choice. She could have run past Arden, gained one position, and finished second to last instead of last. No one would have blamed her. It's a race. You're supposed to pass people.

**She stopped.**

Meghan bent down, draped Arden's arm over her own shoulders, and began to half-carry, half-walk the barely conscious runner toward the finish line. Step by agonizing step, the two teenagers moved forward together.

The stadium fell silent — then erupted. Thousands of spectators rose to their feet. Coaches and parents wept openly in the stands.

And just before the finish line, Meghan did something that elevated her act from merely kind to truly selfless: she **pushed Arden ahead of her**, making sure Arden crossed the line first. Meghan knew that if she crossed first while assisting another runner, there could be questions about the result. She wanted Arden to get credit for completing her race.

**Arden McMath finished second to last. Meghan Vogel finished last.**

She chose last place on purpose.

---

## "Helping Her Was the Right Thing to Do"

In the interviews that followed, Meghan was characteristically humble:

> **"Helping her was the right thing to do. She was a fellow competitor, and she deserved to finish her race. Any of my teammates would have done the same thing."**

Arden McMath's mother embraced Meghan through tears, thanking her for preserving her daughter's dignity on the track. Arden herself, once recovered, expressed deep gratitude, and the two runners stayed in touch long after that day.

The OHSAA honored Meghan with a **Sportsmanship Award**. Officials reviewed the footage and confirmed that Meghan had gained no competitive advantage — in fact, she had willingly sacrificed her own standing. Her result stood, and so did Arden's.

---

## A Moment Seen Around the World

Cellphone footage of the incident went viral within hours. ESPN, ABC News, NBC, and countless international outlets picked up the story. Meghan appeared on morning shows, was featured in sports magazines, and received letters from people around the globe who had been moved to tears by what they saw.

The image of a teenage girl carrying a stranger across a finish line became one of the **most shared sports photographs of 2012**. It wasn't a photo of a victory celebration or a record-breaking performance. It was a photo of a girl choosing kindness over competition.

Yet Meghan consistently deflected the praise:

> **"I won the 1,600 meters that day, but finishing last in the 3,200 is the moment I'm most proud of in my entire life."**

---

## What Meghan Taught the World

In a culture that worships winning — where athletes are measured by medals, times, and rankings — Meghan Vogel reminded us of something we too easily forget: **the measure of a person is not where they finish, but what they do when someone beside them falls.**

She didn't calculate. She didn't hesitate. She simply saw a fellow human being on the ground and refused to leave her there. In that refusal, she became something far greater than a state champion.

She became a symbol of what sport — and life — is supposed to be about.

---

## After the Finish Line

Meghan Vogel went on to graduate and continued running in college. She has spoken at events and schools about the value of sportsmanship, encouraging young athletes to remember that character matters more than any trophy.

The moment on that Ohio track lasted perhaps 30 seconds. But it has echoed for over a decade. It is replayed in classrooms, shown at coaching clinics, and shared by parents trying to explain to their children what it means to be a good person.

On a scorching June day in Columbus, Ohio, a 17-year-old girl gave up last place to help a stranger cross a line. And in doing so, she crossed a much more important one — the line between doing what's easy and doing what's right.

---

*"Every runner deserves to finish."*
*— Meghan Vogel, June 2012*
