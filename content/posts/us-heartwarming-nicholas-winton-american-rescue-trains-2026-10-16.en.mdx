## A Death Sentence at Fifteen Months

In 1998, John F. Crowley and his wife Aileen received the kind of news that no parent should ever have to hear. Their fifteen-month-old daughter Megan was diagnosed with **Pompe disease**, an extremely rare genetic disorder in which the body fails to produce an enzyme needed to break down glycogen in cells. Without this enzyme, glycogen accumulates in the muscles, progressively destroying them — including the heart and the muscles used for breathing.

Shortly afterward, their younger son Patrick received the same diagnosis.

The doctors were blunt: infantile-onset Pompe disease was almost universally fatal. Most children did not survive past their second birthday. There was no approved treatment, no cure, and very little research funding. The Crowleys were told to prepare for the worst.

John Crowley refused.

---

## From Wall Street to the Laboratory

Crowley was not a scientist. He was a graduate of the United States Naval Academy and Harvard Business School, working in finance. Medicine and biochemistry were foreign territories to him. But on the night of Megan's diagnosis, he began devouring every scientific paper he could find on Pompe disease.

He discovered the work of **Dr. William Canfield**, a researcher at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center who was exploring **enzyme replacement therapy (ERT)** — the idea of manufacturing the missing enzyme in a laboratory and infusing it into patients. The research showed promise but was stalled due to lack of funding and commercial interest. Pompe disease affected so few people that major pharmaceutical companies saw no profitable reason to invest.

In 2000, Crowley made a decision that stunned everyone who knew him. He left his stable career and co-founded a biotechnology startup called **Novazyme Pharmaceuticals**, dedicated entirely to developing Canfield's enzyme replacement therapy into a viable treatment.

He had no background in drug development. What he had was an MBA, a gift for raising capital, a talent for negotiation, and the most powerful motivator imaginable: his children were dying.

---

## Racing Against Time

Every day that passed without a treatment meant further irreversible muscle damage for Megan and Patrick. Both children were confined to wheelchairs. Both required ventilators to breathe at night. The clock was ticking, and Crowley knew it.

He threw himself into the work with ferocious intensity, raising venture capital, recruiting scientists, and navigating the labyrinthine world of FDA regulations. In 2001, the global biotech giant **Genzyme Corporation** acquired Novazyme, and Crowley joined Genzyme as a senior vice president to oversee the Pompe disease program from inside one of the world's largest rare-disease companies.

But here, Crowley encountered an agonizing ethical dilemma. The FDA and Genzyme's own ethics board raised concerns about a **conflict of interest**: could a father whose own children stood to benefit from a drug be allowed to make decisions about that drug's clinical development? Ultimately, Crowley was forced to step back from direct involvement in the program he had created. The pain of that separation was immense, but he accepted it — because what mattered was that the work continued.

He channeled his energy into advocacy, fundraising, and public awareness, ensuring that Pompe disease stayed in the spotlight and that the research never lost momentum.

---

## The Miracle of Myozyme

On **April 28, 2006**, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved **Myozyme (alglucosidase alfa)**, the first-ever approved treatment for Pompe disease. It was a landmark moment in the history of rare disease medicine.

Megan and Patrick had already been receiving the enzyme through clinical trials. The results defied every prediction their doctors had made. Their hearts stabilized. The relentless destruction of their muscles slowed dramatically. They did not die at two. They did not die at five. They kept living.

Both children attended school, made friends, and began to imagine futures that no one had thought possible. Megan eventually enrolled at the **University of Notre Dame**, where she graduated — a young woman who had been told she would never see her second birthday now holding a college diploma.

---

## One Family's Fight Changes the World

The impact of Crowley's crusade extended far beyond his own family. Myozyme became available to Pompe disease patients worldwide, transforming a death sentence into a manageable chronic condition for many. The drug has been administered to patients in more than 70 countries.

Crowley's story captured the public imagination. Journalist **Geeta Anand** of *The Wall Street Journal* chronicled it in her book **"The Cure: How a Father Raised $100 Million — and Bucked the Medical Establishment — in a Quest to Save His Children."** In 2010, the story was adapted into a major motion picture, **"Extraordinary Measures,"** starring **Brendan Fraser** as Crowley and **Harrison Ford** as a composite character based on the researchers who made the science possible.

After leaving Genzyme, Crowley continued his mission. He became the CEO of **Amicus Therapeutics**, a biopharmaceutical company focused on developing next-generation treatments for Pompe disease and other lysosomal storage disorders. Under his leadership, Amicus has advanced new therapies that aim to be more effective and more accessible than the first-generation enzyme replacement.

---

## "Someone Had to Do It"

In interviews, Crowley has often been asked why he gave up a lucrative and secure career to plunge into an industry he knew nothing about. His answer has always been simple:

> *"My children were dying, and no one was making a medicine for them. If someone had to do it, why not me?"*

John Crowley is not a doctor. He is not a biochemist. He is a father — a father who looked at an impossible situation and decided that impossibility was not an acceptable answer. He leveraged every skill he had, endured every setback, navigated every bureaucratic obstacle, and built something that had never existed before: a treatment for a disease that the world had all but ignored.

Today, Megan and Patrick Crowley are alive. They are in their mid-twenties. They are living proof that love, when combined with relentless determination, can rewrite the rules of what medicine deems possible.

There are moments in life when the universe presents an immovable wall. Most people turn around. John Crowley picked up a hammer. And he didn't stop swinging until the wall came down.

His story reminds us that the most extraordinary breakthroughs in human history have often begun not in laboratories or boardrooms, but in the hearts of ordinary people who simply refused to accept that nothing could be done.
