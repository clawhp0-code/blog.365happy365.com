# "You Found Our Child"
## A Mother's 32-Year Search and the Detective Who Refused to Let It Go

---

### A Sixteen-Year-Old's Heartbreak

In 1964, in Portland, Maine, a sixteen-year-old girl named **Mary Preble** gave birth to a baby boy. She was unmarried, and in the America of the early 1960s, that was enough to strip her of nearly every right. Unwed mothers were pressured — sometimes coerced — into surrendering their children for adoption. Social workers, hospital staff, and even family members told young women like Mary that giving up their babies was "the loving thing to do."

Mary held her son once. Just once. Then a nurse carried him down the hallway and out of her life. She never learned his name. She never learned where he went. She left the hospital with empty arms and a wound that would not close for more than three decades.

> *"I heard the nurse's footsteps going down that corridor, and those footsteps echoed in my head for thirty-two years."*

---

### The Long Silence

Mary eventually married, had other children, and built a life. But she never forgot her firstborn. Every year on his birthday, she bought a small cake, lit a single candle, and whispered "Happy birthday" to an empty chair.

She wrote letters to the adoption agency. She petitioned the courts to unseal her records. But in Maine, as in most states at the time, adoption records from the 1960s were locked tight. The law did not recognize a birth mother's right to know. Every door she knocked on remained shut.

---

### Detective Dave Hickey

By the mid-1990s, **Dave Hickey** was a veteran detective with the Maine State Police, approaching the end of a career spanning more than thirty years. He had worked missing-persons cases, cold cases, the kinds of puzzles that keep a detective up at night. When he encountered Mary Preble's story, it was not through an official case file. It was personal — a favor, a human impulse.

He looked into Mary's eyes and saw something he could not ignore: the unwavering certainty of a mother who would never stop searching.

> *"When I met a woman who had been looking for her child for thirty-two years, I knew this had to be my last case."*

Hickey began investigating on his own time and at his own expense. After work and on weekends, he dug through dusty hospital records from the 1960s. He tracked down retired agency workers. He cross-referenced church baptismal records, old newspaper birth announcements, and fragments of paperwork that had survived decades of filing and re-filing.

When sealed records blocked his path, he found side roads — former nurses who remembered the era, retired social workers whose consciences had softened with age, church clerks who still kept handwritten ledgers.

After months of painstaking work, Hickey found the thread. Mary's son had been adopted by a family outside Maine. He had grown up, married, and started a family of his own. He was alive and well.

---

### The Phone Call, 1996

Hickey called Mary.

> *"Mary, I found him. Your son is alive."*

For a long moment, there was silence on the line. Then Mary wept — not with grief, but with the overwhelming relief of a prayer answered after 11,680 days of waiting.

Her son, it turned out, had always wondered about his birth mother. He had grown up loved by his adoptive parents, but he carried a quiet question that never went away: *Who was she? Did she think about me?*

When Mary and her son spoke on the phone for the first time, she could barely form words. The sound of his voice silenced, at last, the echo of footsteps in that hospital corridor.

Days later, they met face to face. Mary held him and said:

> *"I never forgot you. Not for a single day."*

Her son held her back and replied:

> *"I always felt it. I always knew someone out there was looking for me."*

---

### "You Found Our Child"

After the reunion, Mary visited Detective Hickey. She took his hand and said:

> *"You found our child. Because you didn't give up, I could keep going."*

Hickey retired shortly after. In later interviews, he reflected:

> *"I solved a lot of cases in thirty years. None of them meant as much as this one. A mother's love — time can't erase it."*

---

### A Larger Legacy

Mary Preble's story became part of a larger national reckoning with the **forced adoption era** in America. From the 1940s through the 1970s, an estimated 1.5 million unmarried mothers in the United States were pressured or coerced into surrendering their newborns. Many were teenagers. Many were given no real choice.

In the years following stories like Mary's, Maine and other states began reforming their adoption-record laws, granting adult adoptees and birth parents greater access to sealed files. Nonprofit organizations dedicated to reuniting families grew in number and influence.

---

### The Birthday Table

Today, Mary still buys a cake on her son's birthday. But the chair across from her is no longer empty. Her son sits there, often with his own children — Mary's grandchildren — beside him.

> *"I waited thirty-two years. It was worth every single day. Now every birthday we have left, we'll spend together."*

Some love is patient beyond all reason. And sometimes, when that love refuses to surrender, a stranger steps forward and opens the door.

---

*This story is based on real events that took place in Maine in the 1990s, part of the broader history of forced adoption in the United States.*
