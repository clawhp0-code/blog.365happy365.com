## A Father's Last Words

In September 1939, fifteen-year-old Gerda Weissmann stood in the doorway of her family home in Bielsko, Poland, watching German soldiers march down her street. Her father, Julius, knelt before her and said something she would carry for the rest of her life:

> *"Whatever happens, never take off your shoes. Without shoes you cannot walk, and if you cannot walk, you will not survive."*

It was the last piece of advice he ever gave her. Within months, the Weissmann family — Julius, Helene, son Artur, and Gerda — would be torn apart by the machinery of the Holocaust. Gerda would not see her parents again.

---

## Six Camps, One Pair of Shoes

From 1942 to 1945, Gerda was forced through a succession of Nazi labor camps: Bolkenhain, Marzdorf, Landeshut, Grünberg, Helmbrechts, and others. She sorted yarn, wove textiles, and endured starvation, disease, and brutality that killed the women around her one by one.

In January 1945, with Allied forces closing in, the SS ordered the remaining prisoners on a forced march westward — the infamous **Death March**. Approximately 2,000 women began walking through the frozen Czech countryside. They walked for nearly four months, covering roughly 350 miles. Those who fell were shot. Those who slowed were left to freeze.

When the march finally ended near the town of Volary in Czechoslovakia, only about **120 women were still alive**.

Gerda, now twenty-one years old, weighed less than 68 pounds. Her hair had turned completely white. But on her feet — swollen, frostbitten, barely functional — were her shoes. She had never taken them off.

## The Door That Changed Everything

On May 7, 1945, a young lieutenant from the U.S. Army's 5th Infantry Division pushed open the door of an abandoned bicycle factory near Volary. Inside, he found rows of emaciated women lying on the floor, many of them already dead.

His name was **Kurt Klein**. Born in Germany, Kurt had fled to the United States in 1937 as a Jewish refugee. His parents, unable to obtain American visas, had been deported to Auschwitz, where they perished.

When Gerda saw the American uniform, she gathered her last strength, stood, and whispered: *"We are Jews."*

Kurt took her hand and answered: *"I am a Jew too."*

Then he did something that shattered three years of dehumanization in a single gesture: **he held the door open for her**. A small act of courtesy — meaningless in ordinary life — that told Gerda she was a human being again.

> *"That moment was my liberation,"* she later wrote. *"Not the end of the war, not the arrival of the soldiers — but the moment a young man held a door open for me and let me walk through it."*

---

## A New Life in America

After months of recovery in a Czech hospital, Gerda and Kurt married in Paris in June 1946. They settled first in Buffalo, New York, and later moved to Scottsdale, Arizona, where they would live for decades as proud American citizens.

In 1957, Gerda published her memoir, **"All But My Life."** The book has never gone out of print. For more than sixty years it has been assigned reading in American high schools and universities, introducing generation after generation to the human cost of hatred — and the stubborn persistence of hope.

But Gerda's greatest contribution was not the book. It was her **presence**. For over fifty years, she traveled to schools, churches, community centers, and military bases across the United States, telling her story in person. She spoke thousands of times, and not once did she say, "Hate the Nazis." Instead, she said:

> *"One act of kindness can cancel a thousand acts of cruelty."*

---

## A Brother Found

After the war, Gerda believed she was the sole survivor of her family. Then, in late 1945, extraordinary news arrived: her brother **Artur Weissmann** was alive. He had escaped through Russia and Turkey and eventually settled in what would become Israel. The siblings were reunited after years of searching — a moment Gerda described as feeling her father's arms and her mother's eyes all at once.

---

## An Oscar and a Medal of Freedom

In 1995, filmmaker Kimberly Mays produced a short documentary called **"One Survivor Remembers,"** which told Gerda's story in her own words. The film won the **Academy Award for Best Documentary Short Subject** and an Emmy Award, bringing Gerda's message to millions.

In 2011, President Barack Obama awarded Gerda the **Presidential Medal of Freedom**, the highest civilian honor in the United States. At the White House ceremony, Obama said:

> *"Gerda has shown us that even in the darkest places, it is possible to find light — and to share that light with others for the rest of your life."*

---

## The Last Lecture

Kurt Klein passed away in 2002 after fifty-six years of marriage. Gerda continued her mission alone. Even in her nineties, seated in a wheelchair, she would face audiences of young students and tell them:

> *"I believe each of you can change the world. Because when one young soldier held a door open for me, he changed mine."*

On April 3, 2022, Gerda Weissmann Klein died peacefully at her home in Scottsdale, Arizona. She was ninety-seven years old.

---

## Legacy: A Pair of Shoes

At the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in Washington, D.C., thousands of shoes are displayed behind glass — a silent testament to the millions who perished. Visitors look at those shoes and think of numbers.

But those who know Gerda's story think of something else: a father's whispered advice, a girl who refused to take off her shoes through 350 miles of frozen hell, and a woman who walked through the rest of her life teaching America that love is stronger than hate.

The **Gerda and Kurt Klein Foundation**, which Gerda helped establish, continues its work today — funding tolerance education programs, community service initiatives, and the annual **Gerda and Kurt Klein Citizenship Award** that recognizes young people who demonstrate extraordinary compassion and civic responsibility.

Gerda's shoes eventually wore out. But her footprints remain — in the classrooms where students first learn what it means to choose kindness, in the communities where strangers decide to hold doors open for one another, and in the enduring truth she carried across an ocean and across a lifetime:

**"I survived to love again."**
