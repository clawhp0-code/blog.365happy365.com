# "If I Did Nothing, I Would Be Consenting to Murder"
## The Story of Irena Gut Opdyke — A Woman Who Hid 12 Jews in a Nazi Officer's Basement

## A Nursing Student Thrust into War

Irena Gut was born on May 5, 1922, in Kozienice, Poland, the eldest of five daughters in a warm, Catholic family. Her father was an architect, and her childhood was filled with books, music, and faith. She enrolled in nursing school, dreaming of a life spent caring for others. But in September 1939, both Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union invaded Poland, and everything Irena knew was shattered.

She was captured by Soviet soldiers, beaten, and sexually assaulted. Sent to a labor hospital near the Ural Mountains, she eventually escaped and made her way back to German-occupied Poland, where she was forced into labor — first at an ammunition factory, then at a hotel and dining hall serving German officers in the town of Ternopil (then in southeastern Poland, now in Ukraine).

---

## The Moment That Changed Everything

In 1942, while working at the officers' dining hall, Irena witnessed a scene that would alter the course of her life. From a window, she saw Nazi soldiers marching Jewish families out of the local ghetto. She watched as soldiers ripped babies from their mothers' arms and bayoneted them. She collapsed, unconscious.

When she awoke, Irena made a vow:

> *"If I did nothing, I would be as guilty as the murderers."*

She began smuggling food under the fence of the ghetto, using her position in the kitchen to steal scraps meant for German officers. She obtained forged identification papers and helped several Jews escape. Every act could have meant instant execution.

---

## Twelve Lives Beneath a Nazi Major's Floor

In 1943, Irena was assigned as the personal housekeeper of Major Eduard Rügemer, a senior Wehrmacht officer. His spacious villa had a large cellar and laundry area beneath the main floor. Irena saw an opportunity — and seized it.

One by one, she smuggled Jewish workers from a nearby labor camp into the underground spaces of the villa. Eventually, twelve Jewish men and women were living directly beneath the boots of a Nazi major, hidden in a network of rooms and tunnels that Irena had carefully prepared.

Every day, she cooked the major's meals, cleaned his house, and smiled politely. Every night, she descended into the darkness to deliver food, water, medicine, and news. Among those hiding was Ida Haller, who was pregnant. Irena helped deliver her baby in secret, muffling the infant's cries to keep the household above from hearing.

The tension was unbearable. A single sneeze, a single misplaced footstep, could mean death for all of them — and for Irena herself.

---

## Discovery — and an Unthinkable Bargain

The secret could not last forever. One day, Major Rügemer discovered the Jews hiding beneath his home. Irena stood frozen, certain she was about to die.

But Rügemer did not immediately report her. Instead, he offered a grotesque deal: he would keep silent about the hidden Jews if Irena became his mistress.

Irena agreed. She later reflected on that decision with painful honesty:

> *"My body had already been violated by war. But my soul was still mine. And my soul said: save them."*

The arrangement lasted until the Soviet Army advanced and the Germans retreated in 1944. All twelve Jews survived the war. The baby born in the basement survived, too.

---

## A New Life in America

After the war, Irena lived in displaced persons camps before emigrating to the United States in 1949. She settled in Southern California and married William Opdyke, a United Nations official. For decades, she kept her wartime experiences largely to herself, building a quiet life far from the horrors of Europe.

Everything changed in the early 1990s. While visiting a local high school, Irena encountered students who claimed the Holocaust had been exaggerated — or never happened at all. Something inside her broke open. She realized that silence was no longer an option.

Irena began speaking publicly — at schools, churches, synagogues, libraries, and civic organizations across the United States. Her testimony was raw, detailed, and devastating. She did not spare herself; she spoke openly about the assault she endured, the impossible bargain she struck, and the guilt she carried.

---

## *In My Hands* — A Story That Reached Millions

In 1999, Irena published her memoir, *In My Hands: Memories of a Holocaust Rescuer*, co-authored with Jennifer Armstrong. The book became a bestseller and was adopted as required reading in middle and high schools across America. It won the Christopher Award and numerous other honors.

In 2009, her story reached Broadway in the form of a one-woman play, *Irena's Vow*, starring Tovah Feldshuh. The production moved audiences to tears and standing ovations night after night.

Israel's Yad Vashem honored Irena as **Righteous Among the Nations**, the highest recognition given to non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews during the Holocaust.

---

## "You Are My Heroes"

Despite the accolades, Irena always deflected praise. When audiences called her a hero, she would shake her head and say:

> *"I am not a hero. I was just a girl who did what her heart told her to do. Any of you could make the same choice."*

She often turned the word back on the young people listening to her:

> *"You are my heroes. Because you are the ones who will carry this story forward. You are the ones who will choose kindness over hatred."*

On May 17, 2003, Irena Gut Opdyke passed away at her home in Yorba Linda, California, at the age of 81. But her story lives on — in classrooms, in libraries, in the hearts of the millions of American students who have read her words and understood, perhaps for the first time, that ordinary people are capable of extraordinary moral courage.

The twelve Jews she saved went on to build families, careers, and lives of their own. Their descendants number in the dozens today, each one a living testament to one young woman's refusal to look away.
