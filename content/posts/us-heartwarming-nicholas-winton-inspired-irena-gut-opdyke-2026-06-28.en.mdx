# "The Tips Are for the Kids"
## The Shoeshine Man Who Donated $202,000 One Dollar at a Time: The Story of Albert Lexie

---

## A Small Shoeshine Box, A Giant Heart

In the lobby of Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh, amid the rush of doctors, nurses, and anxious parents, there sat a quiet man with a wooden shoeshine box. His name was **Albert Lexie**, and for more than three decades, he was arguably the most important person in the building — though he would have been the last to say so.

Albert was born in 1942 in Homestead, Pennsylvania, a small steel-and-coal town about 50 kilometers southeast of Pittsburgh. Born with an intellectual disability, Albert had limited formal education. At fifteen, his father taught him to shine shoes, and that simple trade became his lifelong calling.

---

## The Tuesday-Thursday Ritual

In 1981, Albert began shining shoes at Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh. Every Tuesday and Thursday — without fail — he rose before dawn, caught a bus that took roughly ninety minutes each way, and set up his small station in the hospital lobby. He charged between three and five dollars per shine. On a good day, he might earn thirty or forty dollars total, placing him well below the minimum wage.

But Albert had made a private promise that would define the rest of his life:

> **Every single cent he received in tips would go to the hospital's Free Care Fund.**

The Free Care Fund provided medical treatment to children whose families could not afford it. Albert kept only his base shoeshine fee for his own modest living expenses — rent, food, bus fare. The tips, whether a quarter or a five-dollar bill, went straight into a small envelope that he handed to the hospital at the end of each day.

---

## One Dollar at a Time

Albert's donations were not dramatic. Some days, the envelope held three dollars. Other days, twelve. There were no press conferences, no galas, no tax-deduction strategies. Just a man, a shoeshine cloth, and an envelope.

But the dollars accumulated. Year after year, decade after decade, the hospital's records tracked Albert's contributions with growing astonishment.

By **2013**, Albert Lexie had donated more than **$202,000** — roughly $270,000 in today's value — to the Free Care Fund.

To put that in perspective: Albert never earned more than about $10,000 a year from shining shoes. He lived in a small, spartan home. He did not own a car. He rode the bus in Pittsburgh's brutal winters, sometimes skipping lunch to save money. And yet, over thirty years, his one-dollar and two-dollar tips stacked up into a sum that funded surgeries, medications, and treatments for hundreds of children he would never meet.

---

## Recognition — and Humility

As word of Albert's quiet generosity spread, honors followed:

- In **1996**, he received the **Jefferson Award for Public Service**, one of America's most prestigious awards for community impact.
- In **2006**, the hospital awarded him its **PALUMBO Award**, the first time it had been given to someone who was not a hospital employee.
- In **2013**, **CNN** named him a top-ten CNN Hero candidate, broadcasting his story to millions.

Albert was uncomfortable with the attention. In every interview, he returned to the same plain explanation:

> **"The kids are sick. I'm healthy. I just do what I can."**

He did not consider himself a philanthropist or a hero. He was a shoeshine man who liked children and believed that if you could help, you should.

---

## The Hospital's Heart

The doctors and nurses at Children's Hospital understood something the outside world was only beginning to grasp: Albert was not a sideshow or a feel-good footnote. He was a moral anchor. Surgeons who operated on children with life-threatening conditions said that seeing Albert in the lobby reminded them why they went into medicine. Parents who were terrified about their child's diagnosis found unexpected comfort in this gentle man who asked about their kids by name and told them everything would be okay.

Dr. David Palumbo, after whom the hospital's award is named, once said:

> "Albert embodies what this hospital is supposed to be. Not a business. A place where people take care of each other."

---

## The Final Shine

Albert's health began to decline in his later years. He reduced his hospital visits but continued donating what he could. On **October 2, 2018**, Albert Lexie passed away at the age of 76.

The hospital released a statement that read, in part:

> **"Albert Lexie was the heart of this hospital. What he polished was not shoes — it was our conscience."**

His funeral drew hundreds of mourners: physicians, nurses, hospital administrators, former patients' families, and decades' worth of customers who only then learned where their tips had gone. Many wept — not out of sadness alone, but out of the staggering realization that the quiet man who had knelt before them to shine their shoes had been, all along, standing taller than anyone in the room.

---

## The Weight of Two Hundred Thousand Dollars

In the world of institutional philanthropy, $202,000 is a modest figure. Corporations donate that amount in a single afternoon. But no donation in the history of Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh has ever carried as much moral weight as Albert Lexie's.

Every dollar represented a pair of shoes shined by a man who had every reason to keep the money for himself. Every dollar represented a bus ride through the snow, a skipped meal, a choice made again and again and again for more than eleven thousand days: *Someone else needs this more than I do.*

Albert Lexie never set out to change the world. He set out to shine shoes and help sick kids. In doing so, he left behind a legacy that no amount of money can measure — the proof that greatness sometimes smells like shoe polish, rides the bus, and never asks for anything in return.

**Albert Lexie (1942–2018). Shoeshine man. Quiet hero. The heart of a hospital.**
