# "There Are Still People Inside!" — Emmanuel Mensah, the Immigrant Soldier Who Gave Everything

## A Night of Horror Before New Year's

On the evening of December 28, 2017, at 6:51 PM, a fire erupted in a five-story walk-up apartment building at 2363 Prospect Avenue in the Belmont section of the Bronx, New York City. A three-year-old boy had been playing with the burners on a kitchen stove, and the flames caught a curtain. In a building with no sprinkler system and a stairwell that acted as a chimney, the fire spread with terrifying speed.

Outside, the temperature had plunged to minus 15 degrees Celsius — one of the coldest nights in recent New York history. The brutal cold meant that even the water from fire hoses froze on contact with the building's exterior. By the time firefighters brought the blaze under control, thirteen people were dead, including four children. The New York City Fire Department would later call it the deadliest residential fire in New York City in more than 25 years.

## From Accra to the Bronx

Emmanuel Mensah was born in 1989 in Accra, Ghana. His father, Kwabena Mensah, had immigrated to the United States years earlier, settling in the Bronx. Emmanuel followed as a young adult, driven by a desire to build a life and give back to the country that had welcomed him. He enlisted in the **New York Army National Guard**, completed basic combat training, and had just returned to the Bronx for the Christmas holiday to spend time with his father and neighbors.

Those who knew Emmanuel described him as perpetually cheerful — a young man who carried groceries for elderly neighbors, cracked jokes with the children in the building, and always greeted everyone with a wide smile. He was, by all accounts, the kind of person who made a community feel like home.

## Into the Fire — Again, and Again, and Again

When the fire broke out, Emmanuel was in his apartment. He smelled smoke, stepped into the hallway, and was met by a wall of black haze and the sound of screaming.

He did not hesitate.

**The first time**, he carried an elderly neighbor on his back down the smoke-filled stairs and out into the freezing night air.

**The second time**, he plunged back in and dragged another resident to safety.

**The third time** — before fire trucks had even arrived — he re-entered the inferno and brought out two more people.

By now his lungs were full of smoke. Neighbors outside pleaded with him to stop. Firefighters were on their way. But Emmanuel looked back at the building, where people were still crying out from windows and hallways, and shook his head.

> **"There are still people inside."**

He went back in. He did not come back out.

Firefighters later found his body on an upper floor. He had been searching for more people to save.

## A City Mourns, a Nation Salutes

Emmanuel Mensah's funeral in January 2018 drew hundreds of mourners — neighbors, soldiers in dress uniform, city officials, and strangers who had simply heard his story and felt compelled to pay their respects.

New York Governor Andrew Cuomo posthumously awarded Emmanuel the **New York State Medal for Valor**, the state's highest military honor. The Army National Guard posthumously promoted him from Private to **Specialist**. The New York City Council renamed a section of Prospect Avenue as **"PFC Emmanuel Mensah Way,"** ensuring that his name would remain a permanent part of the neighborhood he died protecting.

Mayor Bill de Blasio spoke at the ceremony:

> "Emmanuel Mensah represents the very best of what this country is supposed to be. He came here as an immigrant, put on the uniform of this nation, and gave his life for his neighbors."

## More Than a Hero Story

Emmanuel's sacrifice resonated far beyond the Bronx. In early 2018, the United States was embroiled in fierce debates over immigration policy, border walls, and who "deserved" to be American. Emmanuel Mensah's story cut through the noise with the simplest and most powerful answer possible: he had given his life.

He was not a native-born citizen. He was not wealthy. He held no political office. He was a young immigrant from West Africa who lived in a modest Bronx apartment, served in his adopted country's military, and — when the moment came — chose to run toward danger rather than away from it. Not once, not twice, but again and again until his body could carry him no further.

The four people he pulled from the fire are alive today. They breathe because Emmanuel Mensah decided that his own safety mattered less than theirs.

## A Name That Will Not Be Forgotten

If you walk along Prospect Avenue in the Bronx today, you will see a blue street sign bearing his name: **PFC Emmanuel Mensah Way.** It hangs near the spot where, on the coldest night of the year, a 28-year-old man ran into the flames for the last time.

His life was short — just 28 years. But in those final minutes, Emmanuel Mensah showed the world what courage truly looks like. It looks like a young man standing in the freezing cold, lungs burning, body exhausted, choosing — freely and deliberately — to go back in one more time.

There are still people inside.

He went back in.
