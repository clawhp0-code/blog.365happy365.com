# "The Moment I Let Go, I Wanted to Live"

## Kevin Hines: The Young Man Who Jumped from the Golden Gate Bridge and Came Back to Save Thousands

---

### A Bright Boy with a Growing Darkness

Kevin Hines was born in San Francisco and given up by his birth parents shortly after birth. He was adopted by Pat and Debbie Hines, a loving couple who raised him in the Bay Area. For most of his childhood, Kevin was outgoing, athletic, and full of energy. But around age seventeen, something shifted. He was diagnosed with bipolar disorder.

Despite medication and therapy, the darkness grew. By the fall of 2000, at age nineteen, Kevin was hearing voices — relentless, commanding voices telling him he was worthless and that the world would be better without him. On the morning of September 25, 2000, he told his father, "Dad, I love you." Then he walked out the door. It was meant to be goodbye.

---

### Forty Minutes on the Bridge

Kevin took a city bus to the Golden Gate Bridge. As he walked onto the span, he made himself a silent promise: *If one person — just one — asks me if I'm okay, I'll tell them everything and ask for help.*

He walked back and forth on the bridge for roughly forty minutes. Hundreds of people passed him. Some noticed the tears streaming down his face. Nobody stopped. Nobody asked.

One woman did approach — a tourist. She handed Kevin her camera and asked if he could take her picture with the bridge in the background. He smiled, took the photo, and handed the camera back. Then he placed both hands on the railing, vaulted over, and let go.

---

### Four Seconds of Free Fall

The distance from the Golden Gate Bridge railing to the surface of San Francisco Bay is approximately 220 feet — about 67 meters. The fall takes roughly four seconds. A person hits the water at nearly 75 miles per hour. Since the bridge opened in 1937, more than 1,800 people have died by jumping from it. The survival rate is estimated at around one percent.

Kevin Hines is one of the few who lived to describe those four seconds. In the thousands of speeches he has given since, he puts it this way:

> *"The millisecond my hands left the railing, I had an instant clarity of mind. I recognized that every single problem I thought was unfixable — was totally fixable. Every single one. Except for the fact that I'd just jumped."*

Instinctively, he threw his head back and managed to hit the water feet-first rather than head-first. The impact shattered two vertebrae in his lower spine. He plunged deep into the cold Pacific, legs paralyzed, beginning to sink.

---

### The Sea Lion

What happened next has been confirmed by an eyewitness on the shore. As Kevin struggled to stay conscious beneath the surface, he felt something large nudge him from below — pushing him upward. His first thought was *shark*. But it wasn't a shark. It was a **sea lion**.

The animal repeatedly swam beneath Kevin's body and nudged him toward the surface, keeping him afloat until a Coast Guard vessel arrived. Marine biologists later noted that while exceedingly rare, there are documented cases of sea lions and dolphins supporting struggling swimmers. For Kevin, the explanation was simpler: *"God sent me a guardian angel, and it had flippers."*

---

### Recovery

Kevin was pulled from the water alive and conscious. At the hospital, surgeons stabilized his fractured spine. Against all medical expectations, he gradually regained the ability to walk. The recovery was long, painful, and uncertain — but he was alive.

During rehabilitation, Kevin made a decision that would define the rest of his life. He had been given a second chance that more than a thousand others never received. He would not waste it.

---

### Cracked, Not Broken

Kevin Hines became one of the most prominent mental-health and suicide-prevention advocates in the United States — and eventually, the world.

Over the past two-plus decades, he has spoken in **more than 30 countries**, addressed **hundreds of thousands of people** in schools, military bases, corporate offices, prisons, and hospitals. His memoir, *Cracked, Not Broken*, tells his story in raw, unvarnished detail. He has appeared in multiple documentaries, including the acclaimed 2006 film *The Bridge* and the 2019 documentary *Suicide: The Ripple Effect*, which chronicles the lives he has touched.

His core message is devastatingly simple:

> *"No one wants to die. They want the pain to stop. And the pain can be stopped."*

Kevin has been a central figure in the decades-long campaign to install a **suicide deterrent net** beneath the Golden Gate Bridge. After years of debate, the net was finally approved in 2014, with construction underway and nearing completion. Advocates credit Kevin's testimony — and his willingness to revisit the worst moment of his life again and again in public — as a decisive factor in winning approval.

---

### "Are You Okay?"

Perhaps the most haunting element of Kevin's story is how close he came to never jumping at all. All he needed was one question. One human being pausing long enough to ask, *"Hey, are you okay?"*

No one did.

That is why, in every speech, every interview, every conversation, Kevin issues the same plea:

> *"Please — ask the person next to you how they're doing. Really ask. And then listen. That one question might save a life."*

He often recounts the stories of people who approach him after his talks, weeping, telling him they were planning to end their lives — and his words stopped them. He keeps those encounters close to his heart. Each one, he says, is proof that the four seconds of terror he endured were not for nothing.

---

### A Life Measured in Lives Saved

Today Kevin Hines is married, a father, and a tireless advocate. He serves on the board of several mental-health organizations and continues to travel relentlessly, carrying the same message he discovered in four seconds of free fall above the San Francisco Bay:

*Every problem is fixable. Every life is worth living. And sometimes, all it takes is one person who cares enough to ask.*

The nineteen-year-old boy who jumped from the Golden Gate Bridge wanting to die has become a man who has helped countless others choose to live. In the cruel arithmetic of that day — a one-percent survival rate, two shattered vertebrae, one miraculous sea lion — Kevin Hines found not just a second chance, but a purpose.

He was cracked. He was never broken.
