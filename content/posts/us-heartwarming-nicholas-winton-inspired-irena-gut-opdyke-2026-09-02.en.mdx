# "This Man's Feet Are Freezing" — The NYPD Officer Who Knelt Down and Gave a Barefoot Homeless Man New Boots

## A Freezing Night Near Times Square

On the evening of November 14, 2012, temperatures in Manhattan plummeted below freezing. Crowds of tourists and commuters hurried through the neon-drenched blocks around Times Square, heads down against the biting wind. Almost no one noticed the man sitting on the sidewalk near 42nd Street.

He was barefoot. No shoes, no socks — just swollen, blistered feet pressed against the ice-cold concrete. Hundreds of people walked past him. Some glanced and looked away. Most never saw him at all.

## A 25-Year-Old Rookie

Officer **Larry DePrimo** was 25 years old and relatively new to the New York Police Department. A native of Holbrook, Long Island, he had joined the force because he believed in service. That night he was assigned to a counterterrorism patrol unit in the Times Square area — a routine deployment that was about to become anything but routine.

DePrimo later recalled the moment he spotted the man:

> *"I could see the blisters on his feet from about 15 feet away. It was freezing out, and this man was just sitting there with nothing on his feet. It was the most heartbreaking thing I'd ever seen."*

Without hesitation, DePrimo walked into a nearby **Skechers shoe store** on 42nd Street. He pulled out his own wallet and paid **$75** — not a trivial amount for a young officer's salary — for a pair of all-weather, insulated boots and a pair of thermal socks.

## On His Knees

DePrimo returned to the man, set the bag down, and then did something that would define the meaning of the moment: he **knelt on the cold pavement** and personally put the socks and boots on the man's damaged feet. He didn't hand the bag over and walk away. He didn't stand above the man and drop the shoes. He got down on one knee, looked the man in the eye, and gently dressed his feet as if caring for a family member.

DePrimo had no idea anyone was watching. To him, it was simply the right thing to do.

But someone was watching. **Jennifer Foster**, a communications official from Florence, Arizona, was visiting New York and happened to witness the scene. Moved to tears, she took out her phone and snapped a single photograph.

## A Photograph That Moved the World

Foster sent the image to the NYPD's official Facebook page with a note explaining what she had seen. On November 27, 2012, the NYPD posted the photograph. Within 24 hours, it had received **over 400,000 "likes."** Within 48 hours, every major news outlet in the country — NBC, ABC, CNN, *The New York Times*, *The Washington Post* — was running the story. The image was shared millions of times across social media platforms worldwide.

What made the photograph so powerful was its unposed authenticity. DePrimo was not performing for a camera. He was entirely focused on one suffering human being. A uniformed officer kneeling on a freezing sidewalk to place boots on a stranger's feet — it was an image that cut through the noise and cynicism of the internet age and reminded people of something elemental: **compassion still exists.**

In interviews, DePrimo consistently deflected praise:

> *"I'm not a hero. I just did what I felt was right. I think anyone in that situation would have done the same thing."*

## The Ripple Effect

The story did not end with a viral photograph. Inspired by DePrimo's act, thousands of New Yorkers donated winter clothing, shoes, and money to homeless shelters across the city. The event sparked a broader public conversation about homelessness in New York, where tens of thousands of people sleep on the streets or in shelters every night, often invisible to passersby.

DePrimo himself said the experience changed him permanently. He kept the **receipt for the boots** tucked inside his wallet, and he would look at it whenever the job became difficult or dispiriting.

> *"Whenever I feel down or frustrated, I pull out that receipt and remember why I became a cop in the first place."*

The barefoot man was later identified as **Jeffrey Hillman**, a veteran who, despite being eligible for housing assistance, had fallen through the cracks of the social safety net. The publicity surrounding the incident brought renewed attention to his case and led social service organizations to reach out to him.

## The $75 Lesson

Seventy-five dollars. In a city where a Broadway ticket can cost ten times that amount, it is a modest sum. But on that frigid November night, amid thousands of indifferent footsteps, **one person stopped**. One person saw. One person acted — not with a grand gesture, but with a small, profoundly human one.

Larry DePrimo's story resonates not because of the price of the boots but because of **the posture in which he gave them**. He knelt. He touched. He treated a man the world had overlooked as someone worthy of dignity and care.

In doing so, he reminded millions of people of a truth we too often forget: **the measure of our humanity is not in what we have, but in what we are willing to give — and how we give it.**

*Larry DePrimo continued to serve with the NYPD and received a commendation from the police commissioner. The photograph taken by Jennifer Foster remains one of the most widely shared images of 2012 and has been cited in numerous studies on compassion and viral media.*
