# "These Are Yours" — The NYPD Officer Who Knelt to Give a Barefoot Homeless Man Boots

## A Cold Night in New York

On the evening of November 14, 2012, temperatures in New York City hovered near freezing. The streets around Times Square buzzed with the usual rush of pedestrians bundled in heavy coats, scarves pulled tight against the biting wind. Hundreds of people hurried past a man sitting on the concrete sidewalk near 42nd Street.

He had no shoes. He had no socks. His bare feet rested on the frozen pavement, exposed to the brutal cold.

People walked by. Some glanced and looked away. Most didn't notice at all.

Then a 25-year-old NYPD officer named **Lawrence "Larry" DePrimo**, patrolling on foot that night, stopped.

## $75 and a Pair of Boots

DePrimo was not a veteran cop with decades of service. He was young, relatively new to the force, and living on a modest salary in one of the most expensive cities in the world. Seventy-five dollars was not an insignificant amount of money to him.

But when he saw those bare feet on that frozen sidewalk, there was no internal debate.

He walked into a nearby Skechers shoe store on 42nd Street. He asked the store manager for the warmest, most insulated pair of all-weather boots they carried. He paid **$75 out of his own pocket**. The store manager, moved by what the young officer was doing, gave him an employee discount.

DePrimo walked back to the homeless man. And then he did something that would soon be seen by hundreds of millions of people around the world: **he knelt down on the cold sidewalk** and personally put socks and then the new boots onto the man's feet.

"These are yours," he said.

He didn't linger. He didn't call attention to himself. He simply stood up and went back to his patrol.

## A Tourist's Photograph Changes Everything

What DePrimo didn't know was that someone had been watching. **Jennifer Foster**, a tourist visiting from Florence, Arizona, happened to witness the entire scene from across the street. She pulled out her phone and snapped a photograph: a uniformed NYPD officer on his knees, gently fitting boots onto the feet of a barefoot man on a cold New York sidewalk.

Foster later sent the photo to the NYPD's official Facebook page with a note:

> *"I didn't know this officer's name, but this was the most beautiful thing I witnessed during my trip to New York."*

On November 27, 2012 — the Tuesday after Thanksgiving — the NYPD posted the photo on their Facebook page. Within **24 hours, it had been shared more than 1.6 million times**. Within days, it had been viewed by hundreds of millions of people worldwide. CNN, NBC, ABC, the BBC, and news outlets across the globe picked up the story.

The world learned the young officer's name: **Larry DePrimo**.

## "I Didn't Do Anything Special"

When reporters found DePrimo, he was visibly uncomfortable with the attention. In interviews, he repeatedly deflected praise:

> *"I didn't do anything special. I just saw a man who needed help, and I did what anyone should do."*

He recalled the moment he placed the boots on the man's feet:

> *"When I gave him the boots, he looked up at me and smiled. It was the warmest smile I've ever seen in my life. I'll never forget that expression as long as I live."*

DePrimo revealed that he kept the receipt from the Skechers store in his locker at the precinct — not for reimbursement, but as a personal reminder. "Whenever I have a hard day," he said, "I look at that receipt and remember why I became a police officer."

## The Man on the Sidewalk

The barefoot man was later identified as **Jeffrey Hillman**, a 54-year-old who had been living on the streets of New York. When reporters tracked him down weeks later, Hillman was barefoot once again. Asked where the boots were, he told them he had hidden them because they were "too valuable" and he was afraid they would be stolen.

Hillman's continued struggles highlighted a deeper truth: a single pair of boots, no matter how warm, cannot solve the complex web of homelessness, mental health challenges, and systemic poverty. But DePrimo's act was never about solving everything. It was about **seeing one person and choosing to act**.

Several organizations and individuals reached out to help Hillman after the story went public. The incident also sparked a broader conversation across America about how cities care — or fail to care — for their most vulnerable residents.

## A Deeper Question

Perhaps the most haunting detail of DePrimo's story is not what he did, but what hundreds of others did not do. On that freezing November night, countless New Yorkers and tourists walked past Jeffrey Hillman's bare feet on the frozen concrete. They saw. Some of them surely felt something. But they kept walking.

It took one person — a young officer earning a modest salary, who had every reason to simply continue his patrol — to stop, enter a store, spend his own money, kneel on the ground, and personally put boots on a stranger's feet.

DePrimo didn't post the photo himself. He didn't tell his colleagues. He didn't seek recognition. He simply did what he believed was right and went back to work.

## The Weight of $75

In New York City, $75 will buy you about fifteen cups of good coffee, or one moderately priced dinner for two. It is, by any measure, a small amount of money.

But on that November night in 2012, $75 bought something that money doesn't usually buy: **a moment of pure human dignity**. A young man knelt before a stranger and said, without words: *You matter. You are not invisible. Someone sees you.*

Larry DePrimo went on to serve quietly in the NYPD. He never sought fame and was always quick to redirect attention away from himself. But the photograph — that single image of a uniformed officer on his knees in the glow of a Times Square sidewalk — endures.

It endures because it captures something we all want to believe is true: that goodness is real, that compassion still lives in the hearts of ordinary people, and that sometimes, all it takes to change the world is **one person who refuses to look away**.

> *"I'll never forget that smile as long as I live."*
> — Larry DePrimo
