## A Freezing Night in Times Square

On the evening of November 14, 2012, temperatures in Manhattan plunged below freezing. Near Times Square, one of the busiest corners on Earth, a man sat on the cold pavement with no shoes, no socks — his feet blistered, swollen, and cracked from exposure. Thousands of people hurried past him. In a city of eight million, he was invisible.

Then one person stopped.

**Larry DePrimo** was a 25-year-old officer with the New York City Police Department, assigned to counterterrorism patrol duty that night. He was relatively new to the force, still earning a modest salary, still learning the rhythms of the city's streets. But when he saw the barefoot man huddled against a storefront, something stopped him cold — colder than the November wind.

"I could see his feet were torn up," DePrimo later recalled. "I knew I had to do something."

---

## Seventy-Five Dollars and a Pair of Boots

DePrimo walked into a nearby Skechers shoe store on West 42nd Street. He approached the manager and asked for the warmest, most insulated pair of all-weather boots they had — and a pair of thermal socks to go with them.

The total came to **$75**. For a young officer living on a starting NYPD salary and commuting from Long Island, it was not a trivial sum. He paid without hesitation.

He carried the boots and socks back outside, knelt down on the freezing sidewalk, and carefully slipped the socks onto the man's ravaged feet. Then he laced up the boots, one eyelet at a time, making sure they fit properly.

He didn't file a report. He didn't tell his sergeant. He didn't post about it on social media. He simply stood up, wished the man well, and continued his patrol.

---

## A Tourist's Photograph Changes Everything

What DePrimo didn't know was that someone had been watching from across the street. **Jennifer Foster**, a visitor from Florence, Arizona, had witnessed the entire scene. She was so moved that she pulled out her phone and snapped a single photograph: the young officer on his knees, gently fitting boots onto the homeless man's feet, the glow of the city lights behind them.

Foster sent the photo to the NYPD's official Facebook page with a brief note describing what she had seen. The department posted it on November 27, 2012.

Within hours, the image went viral. Within days, it had been **shared millions of times** and picked up by every major news outlet in the United States — CNN, ABC News, NBC, The New York Times, and dozens more. International media followed. The photograph became one of the most shared images of 2012, a year that had no shortage of dramatic news.

---

## "I Didn't Do Anything Special"

Reporters tracked DePrimo down. In interview after interview, the young officer seemed genuinely bewildered by the attention.

"I didn't do anything special," he said. "I just saw a man who needed help, and I had the ability to help him. When I saw his feet, I just thought about how I was standing there in warm boots, and he had nothing. No one should be out in that cold with bare feet."

DePrimo said the moment reminded him of lessons from his grandfather, who had always taught him: **"Even if you don't have much, if you have something to give to someone who has less, you give it."**

He also revealed a small, telling detail: he kept the receipt from the Skechers store in his bulletproof vest, close to his chest. "I keep it there to remind myself that sometimes the most important thing I can do on patrol isn't about law enforcement. It's about being human."

---

## The Ripple Effect

The photograph sparked a wave of generosity across New York City and beyond. Charities reported surges in donations of shoes, socks, and winter clothing for homeless shelters. The NYPD honored DePrimo with a commendation. Citizens started informal "DePrimo funds" to buy footwear for people living on the streets.

More importantly, the image forced a citywide — and national — conversation about homelessness, about the people we walk past every day, about what it costs to stop and care.

DePrimo continued to serve quietly with the NYPD. Colleagues said he kept buying food and essentials for people he encountered on patrol, always from his own pocket, never seeking recognition.

---

## Why This Story Endures

Larry DePrimo's story is not about a dramatic rescue from a burning building or a battlefield act of valor. There was no explosion, no countdown, no life-or-death struggle. It was simply a young man on a cold night who decided that he could not walk past another human being's suffering.

He knelt down. He put socks on a stranger's feet. He laced up a pair of boots.

And in doing so, he reminded millions of people of something they had almost forgotten: **that the smallest act of kindness, done without expectation of reward, can move the entire world.**

The photograph from that November night endures not because it captured something extraordinary, but because it captured something we all have the power to do — and so rarely choose to.
