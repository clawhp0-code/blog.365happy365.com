## The Most Beautiful Bridge in the World — and the Saddest

The Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco is one of the most recognized landmarks on Earth. More than ten million visitors walk or drive across it every year, marveling at its rust-red towers piercing through Pacific fog. But behind the postcards lies a darker truth: since its opening in 1937, over 1,700 people have ended their lives by jumping from its railings into the cold waters 220 feet below, making it one of the most frequently used suicide sites in the world.

For most of its history, the people tasked with preventing those deaths were not therapists or crisis counselors. They were California Highway Patrol (CHP) officers on routine bridge patrol. And among them, one officer became legendary.

## Kevin Briggs: An Ordinary Officer, an Extraordinary Mission

**Kevin Briggs** joined the CHP in the early 1990s and was eventually assigned to the Marin County division, which included the Golden Gate Bridge. He had no special training in suicide intervention when he first encountered someone standing on the wrong side of the railing. What he did have was an instinct that would prove more powerful than any protocol: **the willingness to be present and listen.**

Briggs did not rush at people. He did not shout commands. He approached slowly, stood at a respectful distance, and said something remarkably simple:

> *"Hey there. What's going on today? Would you like to talk about it?"*

And then he waited. One minute. Five minutes. Sometimes more than an hour. Standing in the wind and fog, he listened to stories of financial ruin, lost marriages, mental illness, unbearable grief. Over and over, the people on the railing told him the same thing: *"Nobody cares about me."*

Briggs would answer: *"I'm here right now, aren't I? I care."*

Over his 23-year career, Briggs talked **more than 200 people** back over the railing to safety. He became known as the **"Guardian of the Golden Gate."**

## The Day He Met Kevin Berthia

The most well-known moment of Briggs's career came in **March 2005**. A 22-year-old man named **Kevin Berthia** stood on the outer chord of the bridge, staring down at the bay. Berthia was a new father — his baby daughter had been born just weeks earlier — but he was drowning in depression and financial despair. He believed his family would be better off without him.

Briggs spotted him and approached carefully. Instead of asking why Berthia wanted to jump, he asked a different kind of question:

> *"You said you have a daughter. What's her name?"*

Berthia answered. Tears began to fall. Briggs kept asking — about the baby's smile, about Berthia's wife, about the things that once made him happy. For over an hour, two strangers stood together on a bridge above the abyss, one speaking and one listening.

Finally, Kevin Berthia climbed back over the railing.

## A Reunion Eight Years Later

In **2013**, the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention honored Kevin Briggs with a public service award. When Briggs walked to the stage, he was met by a man he hadn't seen in eight years: **Kevin Berthia**, now a father of three.

Berthia handed Briggs the award and said:

> *"If you hadn't spoken to me that day, my children would never have been born."*

The two Kevins embraced on stage. The audience wept.

Berthia went on to become a suicide prevention advocate himself, sharing his story at conferences and schools across the country. He credits Briggs not with saving his life through heroic physical action, but through something far quieter — **genuine human connection.**

## "Listening Is Everything"

After retiring from the CHP in 2013, Briggs dedicated his life to suicide prevention advocacy. In a widely viewed **2014 TED Talk**, he told the audience:

> *"What I learned on that bridge is this: people don't want to die. They want the pain to stop. And when someone sincerely says, 'I hear you, I'm here for you,' many of them choose to live."*

His talk has been viewed millions of times. His testimony and years of advocacy played a critical role in pushing for the installation of a **suicide deterrent net** beneath the Golden Gate Bridge — a project that was approved after decades of debate, began construction in 2018, and was completed in **2023**.

Briggs also authored a book, *Guardian of the Golden Gate*, and continues to speak at law enforcement agencies, hospitals, schools, and mental health organizations nationwide.

## The Quietest Kind of Heroism

Kevin Briggs never fired a weapon to save a life on that bridge. He never leaped into danger or performed a dramatic rescue. He simply stood in the wind, looked another human being in the eye, and said: *"Tell me more."*

That act — the act of listening — pulled more than 200 people back from the edge. It gave Kevin Berthia three children. It gave countless families their loved ones back. And it proved something that the world too often forgets:

**Sometimes the most heroic thing you can do is stay, and listen.**

> *"It's not over yet. Talk to me a little longer."*
