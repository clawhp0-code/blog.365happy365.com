## Born Into Hatred

Derek Black didn't choose white nationalism. He was born into it.

His father, **Don Black**, founded **Stormfront** in 1995 — the internet's first and largest white supremacist forum. His godfather was **David Duke**, the former Grand Wizard of the Ku Klux Klan. Before Derek could drive a car, he was already hosting a white nationalist children's website and appearing on supremacist radio programs. By 19, he had won a seat on the Palm Beach County Republican Executive Committee.

Within the movement, Derek was called **"the crown prince of white nationalism"** — the polished, articulate young face who would carry the ideology into the mainstream. He was everything the movement needed: educated, well-spoken, and convinced.

## A Small College in Sarasota

In 2010, Derek enrolled at **New College of Florida**, a small, progressive liberal arts college in Sarasota. He kept his identity hidden, studying medieval history and earning top grades. To his classmates, he was simply a quiet, intellectually curious student who played guitar and loved European history.

That changed in **2011**, when a fellow student discovered Derek's identity online and posted the revelation on the school's student forum. The message was blunt: *"Have you guys seen this? Derek Black — our Derek — is the son of the guy who runs Stormfront."*

The campus erupted. Students organized protests. Former friends cut ties overnight. Condemnation statements circulated. Derek became a pariah.

## The Invitation That Changed Everything

Amid the outrage, one student made a radically different choice.

**Matthew Stevenson** was the only **Orthodox Jewish** student at New College. Every Friday evening, he hosted a small Shabbat dinner in his dorm room — baking challah bread, lighting candles, sharing food and conversation with a handful of friends from all backgrounds.

When Derek's identity was exposed, Matthew did something that stunned his peers: **he invited Derek to Shabbat dinner.**

Matthew later explained his reasoning in simple terms:

> *"Ostracizing Derek wasn't going to change his mind. I'd never seen anyoneeli­minate hatred by screaming at people. I just thought — let me invite him to dinner and see what happens."*

Derek accepted. And so began one of the most remarkable transformations in modern American history.

## Friday Nights at the Table

Week after week, Derek sat at Matthew's table. He broke bread with Jewish, Latino, Black, and multiracial students. Nobody lectured him. Nobody demanded he renounce his beliefs on the spot. They simply **treated him as a human being** — asked about his classes, shared jokes, talked about music and history and life.

Over months, other students joined Matthew's quiet effort. **Allison Gornik**, a fellow student who became close to Derek, engaged him in long, patient conversations about the scientific claims underlying his racial ideology. She didn't attack him — she showed him peer-reviewed research, population genetics data, and academic sources that systematically dismantled the pseudoscientific foundations of white nationalism.

Derek was, above all, an intellectually honest person. When the data contradicted his beliefs, he couldn't look away. The people sitting across from him at dinner — the people his ideology called inferior or dangerous — were his friends. They had fed him, laughed with him, cared about him.

The cognitive dissonance became unbearable.

## "I Was Wrong"

In **July 2013**, Derek Black published an open letter on the **Southern Poverty Law Center's** blog. His words were unequivocal:

> *"I can't support a movement that tells me I can't be a friend to whomever I wish or that other people's races require me to think of them in a certain way. White nationalism is wrong. I am sorry for the damage done."*

The white nationalist world reacted with fury. Don Black expressed public disappointment in his son. Stormfront members called Derek a traitor. David Duke was reportedly devastated. Derek effectively lost his family, his community, and every social structure he had ever known.

But he gained something he had never truly had: **the freedom to see people as they are.**

## The Ripple Effect

Derek went on to pursue a **Ph.D. in medieval history at the University of Chicago**. He has since spoken publicly about deradicalization, working to help others leave extremist movements.

Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist **Eli Saslow** chronicled this story in his 2018 book ***Rising Out of Hatred***, which became a national bestseller and one of the most acclaimed nonfiction works of the year. The book was later optioned for a film adaptation.

Matthew Stevenson never sought the spotlight. He didn't write op-eds or organize rallies. He simply kept baking bread every Friday and leaving one chair open at his table.

## The Power of a Meal

This story is sometimes compared to that of **Daryl Davis**, the Black musician who befriended KKK members. The parallels are striking, but the mechanism here was different. Matthew didn't go seeking out white supremacists to convert. He simply **refused to exclude someone from his table** — even someone whose ideology called for the exclusion of people like Matthew himself.

Derek Black reflected on this years later:

> *"Matthew didn't change me. He gave me the chance to see the real world. Sitting at that table, looking at actual faces, hearing actual voices — I couldn't maintain the abstractions anymore. The ideology depends on never actually knowing the people you're taught to hate."*

One dinner invitation. One open chair. One act of radical, almost absurd hospitality.

It was enough to break a chain of hatred that had been forged over generations.

In a nation increasingly fractured by suspicion and tribal fury, Matthew Stevenson's Friday night table stands as quiet, luminous proof: **sometimes the most revolutionary act in the world is simply asking someone to sit down and eat.**
