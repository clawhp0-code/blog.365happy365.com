## A Death Sentence at Fifteen Months

In 1998, John and Aileen Crowley of New Jersey received news that would shatter any parent's world. Their infant daughter, Megan, was diagnosed with **Pompe disease** — a rare genetic disorder in which the body lacks the enzyme needed to break down glycogen in muscles. The sugar builds up relentlessly, weakening the heart, the lungs, and eventually every muscle in the body. The prognosis was blunt: Megan was unlikely to survive past the age of nine.

Then their son Patrick received the same diagnosis. Two of their three children were living under a ticking clock with no approved treatment anywhere in the world.

## "I Don't Have Time to Wait"

John Crowley was no scientist. He was a finance executive at Bristol-Myers Squibb, trained in economics and law, not biochemistry. But when you watch your children's muscles deteriorate week by week, when the ventilator hums louder each night, credentials become irrelevant.

Crowley began devouring every piece of published research on Pompe disease. He tracked down **Dr. William Canfield**, a biochemist at the University of Oklahoma who had promising theoretical work on enzyme replacement therapy. Canfield believed a treatment was scientifically possible — but funding was nearly nonexistent for a disease that affected fewer than 10,000 people worldwide.

## Building a Company from Desperation

In 2000, Crowley did the unthinkable. He left his stable corporate job, mortgaged his family's future, and co-founded **Novazyme Pharmaceuticals**, a biotech startup dedicated to developing a Pompe disease treatment. He recruited Canfield, pitched venture capitalists with the urgency only a father could muster, and pushed the fledgling company forward at breakneck speed.

But a tiny startup could not run the massive clinical trials required for FDA approval. Crowley made a strategic — and emotionally agonizing — decision. In 2001, he orchestrated the acquisition of Novazyme by **Genzyme Corporation**, one of the world's leading biotech firms, and joined Genzyme himself to lead the Pompe disease program.

The merger came with a painful caveat. Because his own children stood to benefit from the drug, Crowley faced intense scrutiny over conflicts of interest. He was forced to recuse himself from decisions about clinical trial enrollment — meaning a father whose children's lives hung in the balance could not directly advocate for their inclusion.

## The First Approved Treatment

Years of grueling lab work, bureaucratic hurdles, and sleepless nights culminated in a breakthrough. In 2003, Genzyme began clinical trials for **Myozyme (alglucosidase alfa)**, an enzyme replacement therapy for Pompe disease. Megan and Patrick were enrolled.

The results were extraordinary. Patients showed stabilized muscle function, improved cardiac performance, and — most critically — dramatically extended survival. Children who had been given months to live were celebrating birthdays their doctors never expected.

In **2006**, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration granted full approval to Myozyme. It was the **first-ever approved treatment for Pompe disease**. What had been a certain death sentence became a manageable chronic condition for thousands of patients worldwide.

## Beating Every Prediction

Megan and Patrick Crowley defied every grim forecast. Bound to wheelchairs and reliant on ventilators, they nonetheless grew up, attended school, made friends, and dreamed of college. In **2019**, Megan graduated from the **University of Notre Dame** — the same university where her father had once studied. Patrick enrolled there as well.

At her graduation ceremony, Megan said simply:

> *"If my dad had given up, I wouldn't be here."*

## A Love Story Told on Screen

The Crowley family's extraordinary journey was adapted into the 2010 film **"Extraordinary Measures,"** starring Harrison Ford as a character inspired by Dr. Canfield and Brendan Fraser as John Crowley. While Hollywood took creative liberties, the core truth remained: a father's refusal to accept the unacceptable changed the trajectory of medical science.

## The Ripple Effect

Today, enzyme replacement therapy for Pompe disease is available in more than 50 countries. Thousands of patients who would have died in childhood are alive, growing, working, and raising families of their own.

John Crowley went on to lead other biotech ventures focused on rare diseases, carrying forward the mission that began at his daughter's bedside. He has spoken at conferences worldwide, not as a scientist, but as living proof that **love can be the most powerful catalyst for change.**

The story of John Crowley is not just about medicine. It is about what happens when a parent looks at an impossible situation and says, *"No. Not my children. Not today."* It is about the audacity to believe that one person — armed with nothing but desperation and devotion — can bend the arc of science toward hope.

Every vial of Myozyme administered in a hospital today carries within it the echo of a father's whispered promise:

**"I won't let you die."**
