## A Life Measured in Laundry Loads

In the small city of Hattiesburg, Mississippi, Oseola McCarty (1908–1999) dropped out of school in the sixth grade to care for a sick aunt. From the age of twelve, her days were consumed by a single task: washing other people's clothes. She scrubbed them on a washboard, boiled them in a large iron pot, and hung them on a line to dry — year after year, decade after decade.

Even after washing machines became commonplace, Oseola continued washing by hand. "The machine tears up the clothes," she once explained. "My hands do better." For each bundle, she earned as little as $1.50 in her early years, eventually rising to no more than $10 per load.

## Seventy-Five Years of Saving

Oseola never owned a car. She never had air conditioning. She walked everywhere — to church, to the grocery store, to her clients' homes to pick up laundry. Her greatest luxury was buying a new Bible when the old one fell apart from use.

But she had one unbreakable habit: no matter how small the payment, she always set a portion aside. She deposited coins and crumpled bills into savings accounts at the local Trustmark National Bank. Over the decades, compound interest did its quiet work. By 1995, when crippling arthritis finally forced the 87-year-old to retire, a bank officer informed her that her accounts held **more than $250,000**.

## Ten Dimes and a Decision

When the bank's financial advisor sat down with Oseola to discuss how she wanted to allocate her savings, she didn't need complicated charts. She arranged ten dimes on the table, each representing ten percent of her wealth. She pushed one toward her church. She pushed one each toward three cousins. And she pushed the remaining **six dimes** — sixty percent — toward the University of Southern Mississippi.

"I just want the scholarship to go to some child who needs it," she said, "to get the education I didn't get."

She attached a single condition: priority should go to **African American students who could not otherwise afford tuition**. Oseola knew all too well what it meant to have a door closed because of poverty and race.

The total earmarked for scholarships came to **$150,000**.

## A Nation Takes Notice

When the story broke in July 1995, it spread like wildfire. The New York Times, CNN, NBC, and The Washington Post all ran features. The question on everyone's lips was the same: how could a washerwoman accumulate — and then give away — $150,000?

President Bill Clinton invited Oseola to the White House and awarded her the **Presidential Citizens Medal**. Harvard University offered an honorary degree (she accepted it in 1998). The United Nations honored her with a special award. She was named among Glamour magazine's Women of the Year and received a standing ovation at the ceremony — from an audience that included luminaries who earned in a single day what Oseola had made in a year.

But the most extraordinary consequence was the **ripple effect**. Inspired by her story, people across the country began sending donations to the University of Southern Mississippi's scholarship fund. Business owners, retired teachers, factory workers, and even laundry operators from other states contributed. Within months, additional donations exceeded **$330,000**, swelling the Oseola McCarty Endowed Scholarship to **more than $480,000**.

## Stephanie Bullock: The First Scholar

The scholarship's inaugural recipient was Stephanie Bullock, an eighteen-year-old from Hattiesburg who dreamed of going to college but couldn't afford the tuition. When Stephanie visited Oseola for the first time, the elderly woman took the young girl's hand and said, "I want to see you graduate."

In 1998, Stephanie did just that, earning a degree in business administration from the University of Southern Mississippi. Oseola was in the audience, seated in a wheelchair at age ninety, tears streaming down her face as she clapped. Stephanie later became actively involved in managing the scholarship fund, ensuring that Oseola's legacy would continue to open doors for future students.

## "I'm Richer Than I've Ever Been"

Reporters frequently asked Oseola whether she regretted giving away so much money. Her answer never wavered.

> "I know it won't be too many years before I pass on, and I just figured the money would do them a lot more good than it would do me. I lived a good life. I had everything I needed — my Bible, my church, my friends. That money wasn't for me. I just hadn't found who it was for until now. And now that I have, I'm richer than I've ever been."

## A Legacy Carved in Concrete and Conscience

Oseola McCarty passed away on September 26, 1999, at the age of 91. Hattiesburg renamed the street where she had lived and worked for nearly a century **Oseola McCarty Road**. The University of Southern Mississippi erected a building in her name. The scholarship fund she created continues to award grants to students every year.

Her weathered washboard now sits in the Smithsonian Institution — a monument not to poverty, but to the extraordinary power of quiet, patient generosity.

## What Oseola Teaches Us

Oseola McCarty's life demolishes the myth that philanthropy is reserved for the wealthy. She earned less in her entire lifetime than many Americans earn in a single year, yet her gift transformed lives, reshaped a university, and inspired a nation. She never wrote a bestselling book. She never gave a TED talk. She never went viral on social media.

She simply decided that a stranger's education mattered more than her own comfort — and then she acted on that belief with seventy-five years of discipline, humility, and love.

The woman who never finished elementary school became one of America's greatest teachers. And the lesson she taught is one that no classroom could ever contain: **the size of a gift is not measured by the amount, but by the sacrifice behind it.**
