## Three Days from Graduation

On the afternoon of May 7, 2019, at STEM School Highlands Ranch in the suburbs of Denver, Colorado, an ordinary Tuesday was about to become anything but. Kendrick Ray Castillo, an 18-year-old senior with a passion for robotics and a reputation as the kindest kid in every room he entered, was sitting in his British Literature class. Graduation was three days away. His college acceptance letter — mechanical engineering — was already pinned to his bedroom wall.

Then two armed students walked in, and the world split in two.

## The Seconds That Changed Everything

When the first gunman produced a weapon and began firing, most students dove under desks or froze. Kendrick did something different. He launched himself out of his chair and charged directly at the shooter.

His body collided with the gunman's. In the chaos that followed, two other students — Brendan Bialy and Joshua Jones — joined the struggle, helping to wrestle the attacker to the ground. Those few seconds of resistance gave the remaining students in the classroom the window they needed to flee through the door.

Kendrick was struck by gunfire during the struggle. He was rushed to a nearby hospital, but doctors could not save him. He was the only fatality that day. Eight others were injured; all survived.

Joshua Jones, who was shot twice in the legs while helping subdue the gunman, later told reporters: *"Kendrick was the first one to act. Without him, none of us would have had the courage. He made the choice for all of us."*

## The Boy Everyone Remembered

In the days after the shooting, a portrait of Kendrick emerged that was almost impossibly consistent. Teachers, friends, neighbors, robotics teammates — everyone told the same story about the same person.

He was the kid who sat with anyone eating lunch alone. He volunteered at his church without being asked. He was the one who, during FIRST Robotics competitions, would walk over to rival teams and offer spare parts if they were struggling with their builds. His father, John Castillo, a military veteran, described his son simply: *"He was just good. Not perfect — good. The kind of good where you don't have to think about doing the right thing because it's already who you are."*

Kendrick's mother, Maria Castillo, recalled that he had recently placed an organ donor sticker on the back of his driver's license. He had done it without telling his parents. They only discovered it afterward.

## An Empty Chair at Graduation

On May 20, 2019, STEM School held its graduation ceremony. A chair in the front row was left empty, draped with a royal blue graduation gown. When Kendrick's name was called, the entire gymnasium — students, families, teachers, and community members — rose to their feet. John Castillo walked across the stage and accepted his son's diploma. The standing ovation lasted over two minutes.

The diploma bore a special inscription: **"Graduated with Eternal Valor."**

Colorado Governor Jared Polis posthumously awarded Kendrick the **Colorado Medal of Valor**, the state's highest recognition of heroic action.

## The Gift That Kept Giving

Three days after Kendrick's death, his family authorized organ donation. His organs went to multiple recipients across the country. Months later, the Castillo family received a letter from one of them — a parent who had been on a transplant waiting list for years.

*"Because of your son, I will be there when my daughter graduates. I will never be able to repay what he gave me, but I will live every day trying to be worthy of it."*

John Castillo has spoken publicly about that letter many times. *"Kendrick saved lives in that classroom, and then he saved more lives after he left us. That's who he was — he never stopped giving."*

## A Legacy Built on Kindness

The **Kendrick Castillo Memorial Scholarship** was established shortly after his death. Awarded annually to students pursuing engineering or robotics, the scholarship's selection criteria emphasize not grades or test scores, but character — specifically, whether the applicant demonstrates a pattern of putting others first.

The FIRST Robotics community created the **Kendrick Castillo Spirit Award**, given each year at national competitions to the student who best embodies selflessness, teamwork, and quiet courage.

Brendan Bialy, one of the students who tackled the gunman alongside Kendrick, went on to enlist in the United States Marine Corps. He has said that Kendrick's example that day was the defining moment that shaped his decision to serve. *"He showed me what it means to move toward danger instead of away from it. I wanted to live the rest of my life that way."*

## The Question He Left Behind

Kendrick Castillo's story forces us to confront a question most of us would rather leave unanswered: *What would I do in that moment?*

The honest answer, for most people, is that we don't know. The human survival instinct is powerful, and there is no shame in freezing or fleeing. Psychologists who study crisis behavior emphasize that heroic action under fire is extraordinarily rare — not because people lack courage, but because the body's fight-or-flight response overwhelms conscious decision-making.

What made Kendrick different was not a single moment of superhuman bravery. It was 18 years of small, daily choices — sitting with the lonely kid, sharing spare parts with competitors, putting the organ donor sticker on his license — that built the kind of character which, when the worst moment came, responded with the best of what a human being can be.

At his funeral, John Castillo delivered a eulogy that silenced the room:

> *"The world calls Kendrick a hero. But to me, he was just my boy. The kid who said 'I love you, Dad' every night. The kid who drew robot blueprints in the backseat. My whole world. That he was himself — even at the very end — breaks my heart and makes me proud in equal measure."*

Three days from graduation. Three months from college. A lifetime of dreams within reach.

He set it all down. He stood up.

And because he stood up, a classroom of children is still breathing today.
