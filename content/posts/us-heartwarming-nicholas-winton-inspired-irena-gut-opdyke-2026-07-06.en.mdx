## A Life Measured in Laundry Loads

Oseola McCarty was born on March 7, 1908, in Wayne County, Mississippi, and grew up in Hattiesburg. From the age of six, she watched her grandmother, her aunt, and her mother wash other people's clothes by hand. By the time she was eight, she was helping. By the time she was twelve, she had dropped out of sixth grade to care for her ailing aunt. She never went back to school.

For the next seventy-five years, Oseola McCarty washed clothes. That was her life. She scrubbed, boiled, starched, and ironed—shirts, dresses, sheets, tablecloths—for the families of Hattiesburg. She charged $1.50 per bundle at first, eventually working her way up to $10. She walked everywhere. She never owned a car. She never had air conditioning. Her only luxury was a small black-and-white television set.

But every week, she set aside a little bit. A dollar here, five dollars there. She deposited her earnings into savings accounts at local banks, and the interest compounded year after year after year.

## The Gift That Stunned a Nation

In 1995, at the age of eighty-seven, arthritis finally forced Oseola to stop washing clothes. When a banker helped her review her accounts, they discovered she had accumulated approximately **$250,000**. The banker asked her how she wanted to distribute her estate. Oseola had no close surviving family.

She thought for a moment, then pointed to the portion she wanted to go to the University of Southern Mississippi—a school she had never attended, located just a few miles from the house where she had spent her entire life.

**"I want to give it so that the children won't have to work as hard as I did,"** she said.

She donated **$150,000** to establish the Oseola McCarty Scholarship Fund, specifically to help African American students from southern Mississippi who could not otherwise afford a college education.

## "I Just Want Them to Have a Chance"

When the news broke, it swept across America like a wave. The New York Times, CNN, NBC, and hundreds of newspapers covered the story. Editorial writers struggled to explain how a washerwoman with a sixth-grade education could amass such a fortune—and then give most of it away.

Oseola was bewildered by the attention. In interview after interview, she said essentially the same thing: **"I can't do everything, but I can do something to help somebody. And what I can do, I will do."**

She was not bitter about the life she had lived. She did not regret the decades of physical labor. She simply saw an opportunity to give someone else the chance she had been denied.

President Bill Clinton invited her to the White House and awarded her the **Presidential Citizens Medal** in 1996. The United Nations honored her with a special tribute. Harvard University awarded her an honorary doctorate. The irony was not lost on anyone: a woman who never finished elementary school now held a degree from the most prestigious university in the world.

## Stephanie Bullock: The First Scholar

The first recipient of the Oseola McCarty Scholarship was **Stephanie Bullock**, a young Black woman from Hattiesburg. At the award ceremony, Stephanie met Oseola, took her weathered hands, and wept. Oseola simply smiled and told her, **"Just study hard, baby. That's all I ask."**

Stephanie graduated from the University of Southern Mississippi in 1998 with honors. She would later say that Oseola's gift did not just pay for her education—it changed the trajectory of her entire family.

## A Ripple That Became a Flood

Oseola's generosity shamed millionaires into action. Media mogul Ted Turner publicly credited her example as one of the inspirations behind his $1 billion donation to the United Nations. Donations poured into the University of Southern Mississippi from across the country. Her $150,000 seed grew into a multi-million-dollar scholarship fund that continues to operate today.

Businesses, churches, and civic groups organized matching campaigns. Schools used her story in curricula. Her autobiography, *Simple Wisdom for Rich Living*, was published in 1996 and became a bestseller.

## A Quiet Departure

Oseola McCarty passed away on September 26, 1999, at the age of ninety-one, in the same small house in Hattiesburg where she had lived for decades. She was buried with modest ceremony, but the legacy she left behind was anything but modest.

The University of Southern Mississippi named a residence hall in her honor. The city of Hattiesburg preserved her home as a historical landmark. The Oseola McCarty Scholarship Fund has since helped hundreds of students earn degrees and build careers they might never have imagined possible.

## The Truest Measure of Wealth

Oseola McCarty never flew on an airplane until she was eighty-eight, when she traveled to accept an award. She never ate at a fine restaurant. She never owned anything extravagant. Yet she understood something that many people with far greater means never grasp: **money is most valuable not when it is hoarded, but when it is given purpose.**

Her cracked, calloused hands—shaped by three-quarters of a century over a washboard—reached into the future and lifted strangers into classrooms, into careers, into lives she herself could only dream of.

When asked if she ever regretted giving away her fortune, Oseola shook her head and said:

**"I am spending my money on the children. I don't own a car. I don't have much. But I have enough. And if I can help somebody, then my living is not in vain."**

In a nation that often measures greatness by wealth and fame, Oseola McCarty measured it by something far simpler: whether one person's sacrifice could open a door for another. By that measure, the washerwoman of Hattiesburg may be the wealthiest person who ever lived.
