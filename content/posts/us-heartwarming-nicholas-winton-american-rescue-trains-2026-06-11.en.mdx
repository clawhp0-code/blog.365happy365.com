# "Nicholas's Heart Is Still Beating"
## The Father Who Turned Tragedy into a Gift of Life for Seven Strangers

---

## A Family Vacation That Changed the World

In late September 1994, Reginald Green, a British-born journalist living in Bodega Bay, California, set out on a dream vacation with his wife Maggie and their two children — seven-year-old Nicholas and four-year-old Eleanor. The family drove through southern Italy, soaking in ancient ruins, Renaissance art, and the sun-drenched landscapes that Nicholas had read about in his storybooks.

Nicholas was an unusually curious and generous child. He loved history, devoured books about castles and knights, and had a habit of giving away his belongings to friends who admired them. His father often joked that Nicholas would change the world someday.

He was right — though not in the way anyone could have imagined.

---

## Gunfire on the Autostrada

On the night of September 29, 1994, the Green family was driving along the A3 motorway in Calabria, southern Italy. A car pulled alongside them, and two men opened fire. It was an attempted robbery — a case of mistaken identity, as the attackers later claimed they believed the rental car belonged to a jewelry courier.

Reginald floored the accelerator and managed to escape. But when the family finally pulled over, they discovered the devastating truth: a bullet had struck Nicholas in the head as he slept under a blanket in the back seat.

Nicholas was rushed to a hospital in Messina, where doctors fought for two days to save him. On October 1, 1994, they delivered the words no parent should ever hear: **"There is nothing more we can do. Nicholas's brain has ceased to function."**

---

## The Decision That Changed a Nation

In those darkest hours, consumed by grief in a foreign hospital thousands of miles from home, Reginald and Maggie Green made a decision that would alter the course of medical history in Italy.

They chose to donate Nicholas's organs.

**"Nicholas was the kind of child who would have wanted this,"** Reginald later explained. **"He always shared everything he had."**

Nicholas's heart, liver, both kidneys, pancreas cells, corneas, and arterial tissue were transplanted into **seven Italian recipients**. Among them were Maria Pia Pedala, a 15-year-old girl who received his heart; Andrea Mongiardino, a 19-year-old man who received his liver; and two patients who received his corneas and saw clearly for the first time in years.

---

## The Nicholas Effect

At the time, Italy had one of the lowest organ donation rates in Europe. Cultural resistance, religious hesitation, and deep-seated mistrust of the medical system meant that thousands of Italians died each year waiting for transplants.

The news that an American father — whose son had been murdered on Italian soil — had chosen to give his child's organs to Italian strangers struck the nation like a thunderbolt. The story dominated newspapers, television broadcasts, and parliamentary debates for weeks.

What followed was extraordinary. Italy's organ donation rate **tripled** in the years after Nicholas's death. The phenomenon became known worldwide as **"The Nicholas Effect."** The Italian government overhauled its organ donation laws, and hundreds of thousands of Italians signed donor cards for the first time.

Reginald Green published his memoir, *The Nicholas Effect*, in 1999. The story was adapted into a television film in 1998, starring Jamie Lee Curtis as Maggie Green. The tiny village of Bodiano, near where the shooting occurred, erected "The Bell of Nicholas" — a bell that rings daily in memory of the boy whose death gave life.

---

## A Heartbeat Thirty Years Later

Reginald Green, now in his nineties, continues to travel the world advocating for organ donation. He has visited more than 200 cities and spoken to countless audiences, always carrying a photograph of Nicholas.

One of the most poignant moments in this three-decade journey came when Reginald met Maria Pia Pedala, the teenage girl who had received Nicholas's heart. Now a grown woman with a family of her own, Maria Pia placed Reginald's ear against her chest.

**"Can you hear it?"** she whispered. **"That's Nicholas's heart. It's still beating."**

Reginald wept.

In an interview years later, he reflected on that moment:

> *"The grief of losing a child never leaves you. It's there every morning when you wake up. But when I heard Nicholas's heart beating in Maria Pia's chest, I understood something I hadn't before — that death is not always the end. Sometimes it's a beginning."*

---

## A Legacy of Life

Nicholas Green lived for only seven years, but his legacy has saved and transformed countless lives. The organs he donated directly saved seven people. The cultural shift his story ignited in Italy has led to tens of thousands of additional organ donations over three decades. And around the world, families who heard the Greens' story have made the same courageous choice in their own moments of loss.

More than 130 schools, parks, scholarships, and monuments in Italy bear Nicholas's name. Every year on the anniversary of his death, ceremonies are held across the country in his honor.

Reginald Green has said that he never wanted to be famous, and that he would trade everything — the books, the awards, the speeches — to have Nicholas back for a single afternoon. But since that is not possible, he has devoted the rest of his life to ensuring that his son's gift keeps giving.

**One father's decision. Seven new lives. A nation transformed.**

And somewhere in Italy, in the chest of a woman who was once a dying teenager, a heart that belonged to a seven-year-old boy from California is still beating — thirty years later.

> *"People ask me if I'm angry. I was, for a while. But anger is a dead end. Nicholas's gift — that is a road that goes on forever."*
> — Reginald Green
