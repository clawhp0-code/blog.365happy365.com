## A Life Measured in Laundry Loads

Oseola McCarty was born in 1908 in Wayne County, Mississippi, and raised in Hattiesburg by her grandmother and aunt. She started learning to wash clothes almost as soon as she could stand beside the old iron pot in the backyard. By the time she was in sixth grade, her aunt fell ill, and Oseola dropped out of school to care for her. She never went back.

For the next seventy-five years, Oseola McCarty washed other people's clothes. She scrubbed them by hand on a rubboard, boiled them in a giant black pot, starched the whites until they gleamed, and hung everything on the line to dry in the Mississippi sun. Her clients were mostly white families in Hattiesburg — doctors, lawyers, schoolteachers. They paid her a few dollars per bundle. Even after automatic washing machines became the norm, many customers kept bringing their laundry to her. "Nobody gets clothes as clean as Miss McCarty," they said.

## The Quiet Discipline of Saving

Oseola lived with almost nothing. She never owned a car. She walked everywhere or caught rides to church. She had no air conditioning. Her black-and-white television sat mostly unused. She ate simple meals — beans, cornbread, whatever her small garden produced.

But she had one unwavering habit: every time she was paid, she set a portion aside. Ten dollars here, twenty there, sometimes only five. She deposited the money at the local Trustmark National Bank. She never touched it. Over decades, compound interest did its quiet, invisible work. By the time bank officers sat down with her in the mid-1990s and explained exactly how much she had accumulated, the total had grown to roughly **$280,000** — an almost unbelievable sum for a woman who had never earned more than a few hundred dollars a month.

## "I Want to Give It Away"

In 1995, at the age of 87, Oseola told her banker and her lawyer what she wanted to do. She divided her savings using a simple method: she placed dimes on a piece of paper representing portions for her church, her cousins, and her chosen cause. The largest pile — sixty percent, approximately **$150,000** — she directed to the University of Southern Mississippi to create a scholarship fund for African-American students who could not otherwise afford college.

Her banker asked if she was sure. Her lawyer asked again. Oseola's answer was steady and clear:

> *"I know what I'm doing. I'm giving it away so that the children won't have to work the way I did."*

She had never set foot on the university campus. She had no connection to the school other than the fact that it was in her town, and she knew that education was the one door she had never been allowed to walk through.

## A Nation Responds

When the Associated Press picked up the story in the summer of 1995, it spread like wildfire. Here was a woman who had spent her entire life performing one of the humblest jobs imaginable, who had never earned a large paycheck in her life, giving away a fortune that many middle-class Americans would envy — and asking for nothing in return.

President Bill Clinton invited Oseola to the White House and awarded her the **Presidential Citizens Medal**. The United Nations honored her with a special tribute. Harvard University, an institution she had never dreamed of visiting, conferred upon her an **honorary doctorate**. The woman who left school in sixth grade was now Dr. Oseola McCarty.

She appeared on television, met celebrities, and received standing ovations wherever she went. But through it all, she remained the same quiet, dignified woman who hung laundry in her backyard. When reporters asked how it felt to be famous, she said, "I don't feel any different. I just want the children to get their education."

## Stephanie Bullock — The First Scholar

The University of Southern Mississippi established the **Oseola McCarty Scholarship** and selected **Stephanie Bullock**, a young Black woman from Hattiesburg, as its first recipient in 1995. When Stephanie met Oseola at the award ceremony, the two embraced without a word. Oseola held Stephanie's hands and told her simply:

> *"Study hard, baby. You getting your education is the reason I saved this money."*

Stephanie graduated with honors, fulfilling the promise she made to the elderly washerwoman.

Oseola's gift had a powerful ripple effect. Inspired by her selflessness, donors from across the country contributed to the fund. The scholarship endowment grew from $150,000 to **several million dollars**, supporting hundreds of students in the years that followed. Television mogul Ted Turner, so moved by her story, donated a million dollars to charity and publicly credited Oseola as his inspiration.

## The Last Load

Oseola McCarty died on September 26, 1999, at the age of 91. Arthritis had forced her to stop taking in laundry a few years earlier, but she continued attending church and asking about "her" scholarship students until the end.

At her funeral, university presidents and politicians sat alongside the Hattiesburg neighbors who had brought her their shirts and sheets for decades. The students whose educations she had funded filled the pews. One of them read aloud Oseola's own words:

> *"I can't do everything, but I can do something to help somebody. And what I can do, I will do. I wish I could do more."*

## A Legacy Washed Clean

Oseola McCarty never traveled the world. She never wrote a bestseller or founded a corporation. She spent her days bent over a washboard, wringing out other people's clothes under the Southern sun. But with every dollar she tucked away, she was building something — a bridge between her own lost opportunity and some young stranger's future.

Her $150,000 was not just tuition money. It was proof that generosity has nothing to do with wealth and everything to do with will. It was a declaration that the most powerful investment a person can make is in someone else's chance.

Today, the Oseola McCarty Scholarship continues to send students to college. Her tiny shotgun house in Hattiesburg has been preserved. And her story endures as one of the most extraordinary acts of quiet, radical generosity in American history — a reminder that the size of a gift is not measured in dollars, but in the proportion of one's life it represents.

Oseola gave nearly all of it. And in doing so, she gave countless young people the one thing she wanted most and never had: a chance.
