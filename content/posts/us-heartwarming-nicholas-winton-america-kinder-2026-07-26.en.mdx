## "Dad, I Can Work Too"
### The Story of John Cronin, His Father Mark, and John's Crazy Socks

### A Door That Wouldn't Open

In 2016, twenty-one-year-old John Cronin of Huntington, Long Island, had graduated from a transitional high school program and was staring at a familiar wall. He had Down syndrome, and despite his infectious energy, warm smile, and tireless work ethic, no one would give him a job. Across the United States, the unemployment rate for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities hovered above 80 percent. John was one of countless young adults whose potential was invisible to the conventional job market.

His father, Mark Cronin, was a recently retired business executive. He had spent decades in corporate America, but nothing in his career had prepared him for the heartache of watching his son be told, again and again, that there was no place for him.

One evening Mark sat down with John and asked a simple question: "If you could do anything in the world, what would it be?"

John didn't hesitate.

"Socks, Dad. I want to sell crazy socks."

---

### From Garage to Global

It wasn't a random answer. Since childhood, John had been obsessed with bold, colorful, wildly patterned socks. Stripes, polka dots, superheroes, animals — socks were his chosen medium of self-expression in a world that often struggled to see past his diagnosis.

In December 2016, father and son launched **John's Crazy Socks** from the family garage. Mark built a website; John curated the sock selection. When the first orders came in, John added something no business plan had called for: a handful of candy and a handwritten thank-you note in every package. No one asked him to. He simply wanted the person opening the box to smile.

That small, unrequested act of kindness became the company's signature. Every single order — whether it was one pair or one hundred — shipped with candy and a personal note from John.

---

### Numbers That Tell a Bigger Story

The response was immediate and overwhelming. In its first year, John's Crazy Socks generated over **$1.7 million** in revenue. By 2020, cumulative sales had surpassed **$6 million**, with more than 350,000 orders shipped to all 50 states and 44 countries.

But the numbers that mattered most to John and Mark had nothing to do with revenue.

Of the company's roughly 35 employees, **22 were people with disabilities** — Down syndrome, autism spectrum disorder, cerebral palsy, and other conditions that had locked them out of traditional employment. Each one earned a competitive wage, received benefits, and was treated as an essential member of the team.

John's own title? **Chief Happiness Officer.** Every morning he greeted each coworker with a high-five and a compliment. It was, by all accounts, the most important job in the building.

---

### A Voice on the World Stage

The story of a father-son sock company quickly outgrew Long Island. In 2017, John was invited to speak before members of the United States Congress about disability employment. Standing at the podium, he delivered a message that was brief and unshakable:

> "Give people with disabilities a chance. We can work. We have value."

In 2018, New York Governor Andrew Cuomo spotlighted John's Crazy Socks as a model for inclusive employment. That same year, John and Mark addressed the **United Nations** on World Down Syndrome Day, sharing their story with delegates from around the globe.

The company also committed to donating **5 percent of its earnings** to the Special Olympics and created awareness sock lines for causes including autism awareness, Down syndrome awareness, and breast cancer research, with proceeds going to partner charities.

---

### The Father's Confession

In interviews, Mark has often been praised for his sacrifice — leaving retirement to build a company around his son's dream. He consistently pushes back on that narrative.

> "People say I helped John. The truth is the opposite. John saved me. After I retired, I was drifting, looking for purpose. John gave me one. He gives everyone around him one."

John's mother, Susan, has described watching her son race out the door each morning: "The world told John 'no' a thousand times. I don't think he heard it once."

---

### More Than Socks

John's Crazy Socks is not merely a retail success story. It is a living, breathing argument against the assumption that disability equals inability. Every box that leaves the warehouse carries more than colorful fabric. It carries proof — proof that when you meet a person where their passion lives, extraordinary things happen.

John Cronin still starts every workday the same way: packing boxes, dropping in candy, writing thank-you cards, and high-fiving his team. And when someone asks what a person with Down syndrome can accomplish, his answer hasn't changed:

> **"I can sell socks and make people happy. That's my job."**

A father refused to let the world define his son's limits. A son refused to believe those limits existed. Together, from a garage on Long Island, they built something that no business school could have predicted — a company fueled not by market strategy, but by love, candy, and the world's craziest socks.

Their story continues today.
