# "In That Moment, I Chose to Forgive"

## The Story of Chris Williams — The Man Who Forgave the Drunk Driver Who Killed His Family

---

### February 9, 2007 — A Normal Evening Shattered

It was supposed to be an ordinary Friday night.

Chris Williams, a husband and father of four living near Salt Lake City, Utah, was driving home with his family after a pleasant evening out. His wife Michelle sat beside him. In the back were three of their children: eleven-year-old Benjamin, nine-year-old Sam, and their daughter Anna. Michelle was also pregnant — carrying their fifth child, a baby they had already begun dreaming about.

At an intersection, a car traveling at approximately 70 miles per hour blew through a red light and slammed into the Williams family's vehicle.

The driver was Cameron White, a seventeen-year-old boy. He was severely intoxicated, his blood alcohol level more than three times the legal limit.

The impact was catastrophic.

Michelle Williams was killed. The unborn baby was lost. Benjamin, age eleven, died. Sam, age nine, died.

In the span of a single, violent second, Chris Williams lost his wife, his unborn child, and two of his sons.

Chris himself was critically injured and rushed to the hospital. His daughter Anna and son Michael, who had also been in the car, survived with injuries.

---

### A Decision from the Hospital Bed

When Chris Williams regained consciousness in his hospital bed, the full weight of the tragedy descended upon him. The woman he loved, the children he had raised, the baby he would never hold — all gone.

What happened next defied every expectation of human nature.

Chris Williams chose to forgive Cameron White.

Not after years of therapy. Not after a long spiritual journey. Not after the courts had delivered justice. He made the decision almost immediately — lying broken in a hospital bed, tears streaming down his face, his body shattered and his heart demolished.

"I knew that if I held onto anger," Williams later recalled, "I would lose everything — not just the people I'd already lost, but myself. I would lose the ability to be a father to my surviving children. Michelle would have wanted me to forgive. I knew that in my bones."

He described the moment of decision as the hardest thing he had ever done — and yet, paradoxically, the most liberating.

---

### Facing the Boy Who Destroyed His World

Cameron White was arrested and charged with multiple counts of automobile homicide. The case went to court, and Chris Williams was given the opportunity to deliver a victim impact statement — the moment where the person who suffered the most gets to speak directly to the person who caused the suffering.

The courtroom was tense. Journalists, lawyers, and spectators braced themselves for what they assumed would be an outpouring of justified rage.

Instead, Chris Williams stood before the court, looked at the trembling teenager, and spoke words that stunned everyone present:

"Cameron, I don't want you to spend the rest of your life tormented by what happened that night. I forgive you. I truly do. I don't want this accident to define your life. What matters is who you choose to become from this point forward."

The courtroom fell silent. The judge wiped tears from his eyes. Cameron White broke down and wept.

---

### The Ripple Effects of Forgiveness

Cameron White was sentenced to prison for his crimes — justice was served. But Chris Williams's act of forgiveness planted a seed that would grow far beyond the walls of that courtroom.

After his release, Cameron White dedicated himself to speaking publicly about the dangers of drunk driving, carrying with him the weight of what he had done and the astonishing grace he had been given. He became an advocate, traveling to schools and community centers to share his story, hoping to prevent other tragedies.

Chris Williams channeled his experience into a book published in 2008: **"Let It Go: A True Story of Tragedy and Forgiveness."** The book became a bestseller, resonating with hundreds of thousands of readers who found in Chris's story a mirror for their own struggles with anger, loss, and the seemingly impossible act of letting go.

Williams also became a sought-after speaker, addressing audiences at schools, churches, corporations, prisons, and conferences around the world. His central message was consistent and powerful:

> "Forgiveness is not saying that what the other person did is okay. Forgiveness is refusing to let that event control the rest of your life. It's breaking the chain."

A documentary about his experience was also produced, bringing his story to an even wider audience.

---

### Not a Saint — A Father

What makes Chris Williams's story so remarkable is not that he was some extraordinary figure with superhuman emotional capacity. He was a regular person — a man who coached his kids' sports teams, who worked a normal job, who laughed at dinner and argued about bedtime.

He was a man who, when the worst possible thing happened, reached for something beyond instinct.

Anger is natural. The desire for vengeance is hardwired into human psychology. Every fiber of Chris Williams's being had reason to hate Cameron White, to demand the harshest punishment, to spend the rest of his life consumed by bitterness.

Instead, he chose what he calls "the hardest freedom."

> "Forgiveness is not a single moment," Williams explains. "It's a daily choice. Every morning I wake up and choose again. Some days it's harder than others. But I choose it because I refuse to let that night take anything else from me."

---

### The Hardest Freedom

Chris Williams eventually remarried and built a new life. His surviving children, Anna and Michael, grew up and forged their own paths. The family carries the memory of Michelle, Benjamin, Sam, and the baby who never got a name — not as a wound that festers, but as a love that endures.

Williams continues to speak publicly. He always begins the same way:

> "I'm going to talk to you about forgiveness today. But first, let me be clear: forgiveness is not easy. It is the single hardest thing I have ever done. And I do it again every single day."

His story asks a question that echoes long after the telling:

*If you lost everything — could you forgive?*

And beneath that question lies an even deeper one:

*If you don't forgive — what else will you lose?*

---

Chris Williams's story is not about condoning a crime. It is not about minimizing grief. It is about a man who stood at the crossroads between darkness and light, between a lifetime of justified rage and an almost incomprehensible act of grace — and who chose the path that saved not one life, but two.

His own. And the boy's who destroyed it.

That is the hardest freedom. And it is available to every single one of us.
