## A Single Cabbage That Changed Everything

Some of the most extraordinary stories begin in the most ordinary places. This one started in a third-grade classroom, with a tiny seedling in a plastic cup.

---

### A Seedling Comes Home

In 2008, **Katie Stagliano** was a nine-year-old student in Summerville, South Carolina. Through the **Bonnie Plants Cabbage Program** — a national initiative that distributes free cabbage seedlings to third graders across the United States — Katie received a single small plant. The program's goal was simple: teach children about gardening, and award a scholarship to the student who grew the biggest cabbage in each state.

Katie planted her seedling in the backyard. She watered it every morning, shielded it from pests, and watched with growing amazement as it swelled far beyond anything she had imagined. By the time it was ready for harvest, the cabbage weighed an astonishing **40 pounds (about 18 kilograms)** — nearly half the girl's own weight.

### "Can We Give It to People Who Are Hungry?"

Standing in front of the enormous vegetable, Katie faced a choice. She could enter it in a contest. She could show it off at school. She could take a photo and call it a fun experiment. Instead, she turned to her father, John Stagliano, and asked a question that would alter the trajectory of her life.

> "Dad, can we donate this to people who don't have enough to eat?"

John drove his daughter to a local soup kitchen operated by **Tri-County Family Ministries**. The kitchen staff turned that single cabbage into soup and coleslaw and served it that evening. **Two hundred and seventy-five people** — homeless individuals, low-income families, elderly residents living alone — sat down to a warm meal made possible by one child's garden.

Katie watched them eat. She saw the smiles, the gratitude, the empty bowls held out for seconds. And right there, in that fluorescent-lit dining hall, a seed of a different kind took root.

### From Backyard to Movement

Back home, Katie did not stop. She converted more of the family yard into garden beds. She planted tomatoes, cucumbers, carrots, peppers, squash, and more cabbage — always more cabbage. Every harvest went to local shelters and food pantries.

By 2010, at just eleven years old, Katie formally established **Katie's Krops**, a registered 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization. Its mission was elegantly simple:

> *Kids growing food and giving it to people in need.*

Katie designed a grant program for young people across the country. Any child could apply for a Katie's Krops garden grant, and if approved, they would receive funding for seeds, soil, tools, and mentorship. Each garden would be managed by the young grower, and every ounce of produce would be donated — never sold.

### A Garden in Every State

The idea spread with remarkable speed. Children from Florida to New York, from Texas to Illinois, began planting their own Katie's Krops gardens. By 2023, the organization supported **over 100 gardens across 33 states**, all tended by youth volunteers, many of them elementary and middle school students.

The cumulative harvest has been staggering: **hundreds of thousands of pounds of fresh vegetables** delivered free of charge to soup kitchens, homeless shelters, food banks, and families in need. In addition, Katie's Krops hosts regular **free community dinners** — open to anyone, no questions asked — where meals are prepared entirely from produce grown in the kids' gardens.

### A Nation Takes Notice

Recognition followed. The **Clinton Foundation** honored Katie with its Global Citizen Award. She appeared on **NBC Nightly News**, **CNN**, and **The Ellen DeGeneres Show**. She received the prestigious **Jefferson Award for Public Service**, often called the "Nobel Prize for community service." In 2012, she was selected for the **Oprah Winfrey Network's Super Soul Sunday** segment.

Through it all, Katie remained disarmingly humble. In interviews, she consistently deflected attention from herself back to the mission:

> *"I just like being in the garden. Knowing that something I grew ended up on someone's plate who really needed it — that's the best feeling in the world."*

### The Scale of the Problem — and the Power of One

Katie's work exists against a sobering backdrop. According to the USDA, approximately **34 million Americans** — including 9 million children — experience food insecurity each year. For many communities, access to fresh fruits and vegetables is a luxury, not a given.

A 40-pound cabbage, by itself, cannot solve systemic hunger. Katie never claimed it could. But what she demonstrated — and what continues to ripple outward through every garden her organization plants — is that **compassion is not constrained by age, and generosity does not require wealth.**

Each garden is a classroom. Children learn biology, ecology, nutrition, responsibility, and empathy — all by getting their hands in the dirt. And every donated tomato or head of lettuce carries an invisible message: *Someone cared enough to grow this for you.*

### Still Growing

Now a college student, Katie continues to oversee Katie's Krops while pursuing her studies. The organization's waiting list for garden grants grows longer each year. New gardens are breaking ground every season. Former kid growers who started their gardens at age eight or nine are now mentoring the next generation of young farmers.

Katie still tends her original garden in Summerville. She still delivers produce to the same soup kitchen where, years ago, a wide-eyed nine-year-old watched 275 strangers share a meal made from one cabbage.

> *"You don't have to wait until you're an adult to make a difference. You can start right now, right where you are."*

---

One seedling. One child's question. **"Can we give it to people who are hungry?"**

That question is still growing — in over a hundred gardens, in thirty-three states, in every bag of fresh vegetables carried through the doors of a shelter by small, dirt-stained hands.

Some seeds, once planted, never stop bearing fruit.
