## A Routine Reconnaissance Mission

On the morning of March 16, 1968, Warrant Officer **Hugh Thompson Jr.**, a 25-year-old helicopter pilot from Stone Mountain, Georgia, lifted off on what was supposed to be a routine reconnaissance mission over Quảng Ngãi Province, Vietnam. His job was to fly low over the village of Sơn Mỹ — known to Americans as My Lai — and draw out enemy fire so ground troops from Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry Regiment, could engage the Viet Cong.

What Thompson saw from the air was not a battle. It was a slaughter.

## Bodies Everywhere, No Enemy in Sight

As Thompson circled the village in his OH-23 Raven light observation helicopter, he noticed bodies — dozens of them — scattered along irrigation ditches, pathways, and village huts. They were women. Children. Elderly men. There was no return fire. No sign of enemy combatants. American soldiers were methodically executing unarmed civilians.

Thompson initially tried to rationalize what he was seeing. He marked wounded civilians with green smoke, the standard signal for ground medics to assist. Instead, he watched in horror as soldiers walked up to the people he had marked and shot them.

"We kept flying back and forth," Thompson later recalled, "and the bodies kept piling up."

## A Line in the Dirt

Then Thompson spotted a ditch filled with more than a hundred bodies — men, women, children — many still moving, some crying out. Nearby, a squad of American soldiers was approaching another group of terrified civilians huddled together.

Thompson made a decision that would define his life.

He landed his helicopter between the American soldiers and the Vietnamese civilians. He turned to his crew chief, **Glenn Andreotta**, and his door gunner, **Lawrence Colburn**, both barely out of their teens, and gave an order unlike any issued on an American battlefield:

> *"If those soldiers fire on these people while I'm getting them out, you fire on those soldiers."*

An American pilot had just ordered his crew to aim their weapons at fellow Americans.

Thompson confronted the officer on the ground, a lieutenant, and told him he was going to evacuate the civilians. The lieutenant protested. Thompson didn't waver. He called in two Huey gunships to assist with the evacuation. One by one, the civilians — roughly 10 to 12 from that group — were airlifted to safety.

Glenn Andreotta, meanwhile, waded into the ditch of bodies and pulled out a small child who was still alive, clinging to his dead mother. The boy's name was **Do Hoa**. He would survive.

Thompson then radioed his superiors and reported what was happening. His intervention contributed to the order that ultimately halted the operation. By that point, an estimated **504 Vietnamese civilians** had been killed.

## The Cost of Conscience

When Thompson returned to base and reported the massacre up his chain of command, the military responded — not with an investigation into the killings, but with an effort to silence Thompson. The massacre was covered up for over a year.

When investigative journalist Seymour Hersh broke the story in November 1969, America was convulsed. But the outrage was complicated. Many Americans viewed Thompson not as a hero but as a **traitor** — a soldier who had turned on his own.

Thompson received death threats. Dead animals were left on his doorstep. A congressman publicly stated that Thompson, not the perpetrators, should be court-martialed. He was shunned by parts of the military establishment.

The commanding officer of the massacre, **Lieutenant William Calley**, was convicted of murdering 22 civilians and sentenced to life in prison. President Nixon intervened, and Calley served just three and a half years of house arrest before being freed.

Thompson struggled with survivor's guilt, nightmares, alcohol, and a broken marriage. For three decades, he lived in relative obscurity, working as a helicopter pilot instructor at Fort Rucker, Alabama.

## Thirty Years Late

In 1998 — thirty years after My Lai — the U.S. Army finally awarded Thompson, Colburn, and Andreotta (posthumously; he had been killed in combat just three weeks after My Lai) the **Soldier's Medal**, the highest award for bravery not involving conflict with an enemy.

At the ceremony, Thompson said quietly:

> *"I'm not a hero. I did what was right. The heroes are the people who were in that ditch."*

## Return to My Lai

In 2001, Thompson and Colburn returned to Vietnam. They visited the My Lai memorial, where the names of 504 victims are inscribed on a stone wall. And they met survivors — including **Do Hoa**, the child Glenn Andreotta had pulled from the ditch of corpses 33 years earlier. Do Hoa was now a grown man with a family of his own.

Thompson and Do Hoa embraced. No translator was needed.

"There are no words for that moment," Colburn later said. "Just tears."

Thompson visited Vietnam again several times, building friendships with survivors and their families. He told audiences around the world that moral courage — the willingness to do what is right even when it costs everything — is the highest form of bravery.

## Legacy

Hugh Thompson Jr. died on January 6, 2006, at the age of 62. Lawrence Colburn passed away in 2016.

Today, Thompson's story is taught at West Point and the U.S. Naval Academy as the defining case study in **moral courage**. His decision — made in seconds, under unimaginable pressure, at the age of 25 — saved an untold number of lives and preserved something that was on the verge of being lost entirely that day: the possibility that even in war's darkest chapter, a human being can choose to remain human.

Thompson never regretted his choice. Not once. When asked near the end of his life whether he would do it again, he didn't hesitate:

> *"Yes. It was the right thing to do. And I would do it every single time."*

Sometimes the greatest courage is not pulling the trigger. It is **refusing to**.
