## An Ordinary Life, Interrupted

In the fall of 2012, Kara Tippetts — a thirty-six-year-old mother of four living in Colorado Springs — discovered a lump in her breast. She and her husband Jason led a small church community. Their life was full of school runs, shared meals, and the beautiful monotony of raising children. A biopsy confirmed breast cancer. After surgery and chemotherapy, doctors declared her cancer-free.

The relief lasted barely a year. In late 2013, the cancer returned — stage IV, metastasized to her brain, bones, and liver. The prognosis was devastating.

Kara later wrote about the moment she received the news:

> *"I cried. And when the crying stopped, I picked up my kids from school and made dinner. Because that was the most faithful thing I could do today."*

---

## Mundane Faithfulness

Kara named her blog **"Mundane Faithfulness."** The phrase was deliberate. She was not interested in grand heroics or bucket-list adventures. She believed that love is most powerfully expressed in the small, repeated, often invisible acts of daily life — packing lunches, sipping coffee with her husband, bringing soup to a neighbor.

Her writing was raw, honest, and luminous. She described pain without self-pity. She confessed fear without losing gratitude. She wrote about losing her hair, about explaining death to her young children, about the strange grace that sometimes visited her at 3 a.m. when sleep would not come.

The blog went viral. Millions of readers around the world found in Kara's words a language for their own suffering — and their own hope.

In 2014, she published *The Hardest Peace: Expecting Grace in the Midst of Life's Hard.* It became an immediate bestseller, offering comfort to cancer patients, caregivers, and anyone wrestling with the fragility of life.

## A Letter to Brittany Maynard

In October 2014, America was consumed by a national debate over end-of-life choices. Brittany Maynard, a twenty-nine-year-old woman with terminal brain cancer, publicly announced her decision to end her life through Oregon's Death with Dignity Act. Opinions raged on both sides.

Kara Tippetts chose a different path. She wrote an open letter to Brittany — not with judgment, not with politics, but with the tender authority of a woman walking the same road.

> *"Brittany, I know how great your pain is. I am on this same road. But I beg you — don't rush the final days. There is love in those days. There are unexpected laughs, there are last embraces, there are moments when you get to look into the eyes of someone you love one more time."*

The letter was shared millions of times within hours. CNN, NBC, ABC, and dozens of other outlets covered the story. The national conversation shifted — from "how to die" to "how to live until the very end."

## The Last Christmas

By the winter of 2014, Kara could no longer walk. Her community in Colorado Springs rallied around the family. Church members created a rotating schedule to care for the children. Strangers left food and flowers at the front door. Tens of thousands of letters poured in from across the country.

Kara wrote letters to each of her four children — letters sealed for future milestones. A letter for graduation day. A letter for their wedding day. A letter for the day they would hold their first child. She was packaging her love into time capsules, ensuring her presence would be felt long after her body was gone.

Jason later said in an interview:

> *"Kara was not afraid of dying. The only thing she feared was that her children might forget how much their mother loved them."*

---

## March 22, 2015

Kara Tippetts passed away on March 22, 2015, at the age of thirty-eight, in her Colorado Springs home, surrounded by Jason and their four children.

Her final blog post was written by Jason:

> *"Kara is in the arms of Jesus. The pain is over. And we are going to be okay — because she taught us how to be okay."*

Thousands attended her memorial service. People who had never met her stood in line holding flowers. Her blog received more than two million visits on the day of her funeral alone.

---

## A Legacy of Ordinary Love

After Kara's death, her message continued to spread. *The Hardest Peace* kept selling. Her second book, *Just Show Up* — co-written with her friend Ann Voskamp — was published posthumously and became another bestseller. The phrase "mundane faithfulness" became a movement within American churches and cancer communities.

Every March, a small gathering is held in Colorado Springs to honor Kara's memory. Attendees don't plan grand gestures. Instead, they cook soup for a neighbor, take an extra walk with their children, and say "I love you" one more time before bed.

What Kara Tippetts taught was breathtakingly simple:

**The most extraordinary legacy is not a heroic final act. It is the faithful, loving, ordinary Tuesday — repeated until there are no more Tuesdays left.**

Perhaps today, when you help your child with homework, hand a colleague a cup of coffee, or whisper "I love you" before the lights go out, you are living exactly what Kara called mundane faithfulness.
