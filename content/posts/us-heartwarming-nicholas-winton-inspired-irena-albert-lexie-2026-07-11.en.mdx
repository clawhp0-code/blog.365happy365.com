# "I Just Did What I Could"
## Albert Lexie, the Shoe Shiner Who Donated Over $200,000 in Tips to Sick Children

---

## A Man with Almost Nothing — Except a Purpose

Albert Lexie was born in 1942 near Monroeville, Pennsylvania. Born with an intellectual disability, he received very little formal education and began shining shoes at the age of 15. It was the only trade he ever knew.

A shoe shine cost $3, later raised to $5. Tips were a dollar or two at most. On a good day, Albert might earn $10 to $15. By every measurable standard in American society, Albert Lexie was a poor man.

But Albert had a plan the world didn't know about yet.

---

## 1981: A Decision That Would Span Three Decades

In 1981, Albert learned about the **Free Care Fund** at Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh of UPMC. The fund existed to cover medical costs for families who couldn't afford treatment for their sick children — surgeries, therapies, and life-saving procedures that insurance wouldn't cover or that uninsured families simply couldn't pay for.

Albert made a quiet, extraordinary decision: **he would donate every cent of his tips to the fund.**

Not some of his tips. Not the tips from a good week. *Every tip. Every time.*

From that day on, Albert's routine became almost sacred. Every Tuesday and Thursday, he would wake early, pick up his wooden shoe-shine box, walk to the bus stop, and ride over an hour to the hospital. He'd set up in the lobby and shine the shoes of doctors, nurses, hospital staff, and visitors. The base fee for the shine — $3 to $5 — he kept for his own modest living expenses: rent, food, bus fare. Every tip went directly into the Free Care Fund collection.

---

## Rain, Snow, or Shine — Albert Showed Up

For 33 years, Albert rarely missed a day. He didn't own a car. He lived in a small, humble home. He never married. His life was stripped of every luxury most Americans take for granted.

When people asked him why he gave away his tips — money he clearly needed himself — Albert always gave the same answer:

> **"It's not right for kids to not get help just because their parents can't afford it."**

The math seems almost impossible. A few dollars in tips per shine. A handful of shines per day, two days a week. How could that ever amount to anything significant?

But Albert understood something that Wall Street analysts and financial planners often forget: **consistency, sustained by love, creates miracles.**

One year, a few hundred dollars. Five years, a few thousand. Ten years, tens of thousands.

By the time Albert's health forced him to slow down in 2013, his cumulative donations had surpassed **$202,000** — well over a quarter million dollars in today's value when adjusted for inflation across those decades.

All from shoe-shine tips.

---

## The Hospital's Most Devoted Donor

Inside Children's Hospital, Albert became a legend. Doctors and nurses who had perfectly clean shoes would seek him out for a shine, just to give him a bigger tip. They knew where every cent was going.

But Albert never sought attention. He didn't do interviews. He didn't start a social media campaign. He sat on his small stool, bent over one shoe at a time, brushed, buffed, and polished — and then quietly slipped the tip into the fund.

In 2006, the hospital honored Albert with its **Community Hero Award**. He showed up in his work clothes, smiled shyly, and said:

> **"I'm not a special person. I just did what I could."**

In 2013, Albert was nominated as a **CNN Hero**, and his story finally reached a national audience. People from across the country were astonished — not just by the amount he had given, but by the quiet, stubborn faithfulness of his giving. He had never missed a beat. He had never asked for recognition. He had simply shown up, shined shoes, and given.

---

## The Last Shine

As Albert aged and his health declined, the trips to the hospital became harder. The bus ride was long. His knees ached. His hands, after nearly 60 years of gripping brushes and rags, were worn. But he kept going as long as his body allowed.

On **October 4, 2018**, Albert Lexie passed away at the age of 76.

His funeral was attended by hundreds of people — doctors who had known him for decades, nurses who had chatted with him every Tuesday and Thursday, administrators who had watched the Free Care Fund grow dollar by dollar, and, most importantly, families whose children had received treatment because of Albert's donations.

Some of those children were now adults. Some were parents themselves. They came to say goodbye to the man who had shined shoes so their surgeries could happen, so their hospital stays could be covered, so their lives could continue.

Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh released a statement:

> **"Albert Lexie was one of the most dedicated donors in the history of our hospital. He had the least to give, yet he gave the most."**

---

## The Arithmetic of Love

Albert Lexie's story defies the logic of wealth and philanthropy. In a country where hospitals name wings after billionaire donors, where charity galas feature celebrities in designer gowns, one man with a wooden shoe-shine box and a bus pass accomplished something that money alone cannot buy: **proof that generosity is not about what you have, but about what you're willing to give.**

He earned minimum wage. He lived below the poverty line for most of his life. And yet, over 33 years, he donated more than $200,000 to sick children — one tip at a time, one shoe at a time, one bus ride at a time.

Albert didn't just shine shoes. He polished the very idea of what it means to be human.

> *"I'm not a special person. I just did what I could."*

Perhaps that's exactly what makes someone special.
