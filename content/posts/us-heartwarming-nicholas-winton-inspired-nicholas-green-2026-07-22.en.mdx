# "I Can't Just Watch My Children Die"
## The Father Who Created a Cure for His Kids: The John Crowley Story

---

### A Diagnosis That Changed Everything

In 1998, John F. Crowley and his wife Aileen were living a comfortable life in New Jersey. John was a Harvard MBA graduate building a promising career in business consulting. Their first son, John Jr., was healthy and growing. Life seemed full of promise.

Then their daughter Megan was born, and everything shattered.

Shortly after birth, Megan was diagnosed with **infantile Pompe disease**, an extremely rare genetic disorder in which the body lacks an enzyme called acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA). Without this enzyme, glycogen accumulates in muscle cells, progressively destroying them. The heart enlarges dangerously. Breathing muscles weaken. Movement fades.

In 1998, there was no treatment. No cure. No hope. Doctors told the Crowleys what they told every family that received this diagnosis:

*"Most children with infantile Pompe disease do not survive past the age of two."*

The devastation doubled when their third child, Patrick, born later that same year, received the identical diagnosis. Two of their three children were dying of the same incurable disease.

---

### A Father Refuses to Accept the Verdict

John Crowley did not collapse into despair — he channeled it. Night after night, while his children slept connected to monitors and breathing machines, he read medical journals, research papers, anything he could find about Pompe disease.

He discovered that a handful of researchers were exploring **enzyme replacement therapy (ERT)** — the idea of manufacturing the missing enzyme in a lab and infusing it into patients. Dr. William Canfield at the University of Oklahoma and Dr. Yuan-Tsong Chen at Duke University were among the pioneers. But their work was stalled. Pompe disease affected so few people that pharmaceutical companies considered it commercially unviable. No major company wanted to invest.

Crowley made a decision that stunned everyone around him.

**He would build the company himself.**

In 2000, he co-founded **Novazyme Pharmaceuticals**, a biotech startup dedicated to developing an enzyme replacement therapy for Pompe disease. He recruited Dr. Canfield, courted investors, and threw himself into the biotech world with zero background in science. What he lacked in scientific expertise, he made up for with an asset no investor could quantify:

**The desperate urgency of a father racing against time.**

Megan's heart had swollen to three times its normal size. Patrick couldn't hold up his head. Every passing week brought the children closer to the fate their doctors had predicted.

---

### The Genzyme Merger and Ethical Crossroads

In 2001, the major biotech firm **Genzyme Corporation** acquired Novazyme for over $100 million. Crowley joined Genzyme and became a driving force behind the Pompe disease program. However, an ethical dilemma soon emerged: Crowley's own children would be candidates for the clinical trials of the drug he was helping develop. To maintain the integrity of the research, Crowley stepped back from direct involvement in the clinical program.

It was agonizing. The father who had started a company to save his children now had to trust others to finish the work.

But the science moved forward.

---

### The Miracle of Myozyme

In 2003, Megan and Patrick enrolled in clinical trials for the enzyme replacement therapy. The results exceeded cautious expectations.

Megan's heart began to shrink toward normal size. Patrick's muscle tone showed measurable improvement. Both children remained dependent on wheelchairs and ventilators, but they were **alive** — years beyond what any doctor had predicted.

On **April 28, 2006**, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved **Myozyme (alglucosidase alfa)** as the first-ever treatment for Pompe disease. It was a landmark moment not only for the Crowley family but for the entire rare disease community. The drug was subsequently approved in Europe and across the globe, transforming a death sentence into a manageable condition for thousands of patients.

---

### Beyond Survival: Megan's Triumph

Megan Crowley defied every prognosis. She grew up, attended school, and in **2019 graduated from the University of Notre Dame** — one of America's most prestigious universities. The image of Megan crossing the stage in her wheelchair, diploma in hand, became a symbol of what is possible when science, love, and refusal to surrender converge.

Patrick, too, continued his education and his life — a life that was never supposed to extend past toddlerhood.

---

### A Legacy That Keeps Growing

John Crowley did not stop after Myozyme. He went on to become CEO of **Amicus Therapeutics**, a biopharmaceutical company focused on developing treatments for rare and orphan diseases, including Fabry disease and Batten disease. He has dedicated his entire professional life to the principle that **no disease is too rare to fight for**.

His story was adapted into the 2010 film **"Extraordinary Measures,"** starring Brendan Fraser as Crowley and Harrison Ford as a composite character based on the researchers involved. The film brought global attention to the challenges of rare disease research and the families who live with these conditions every day.

---

### What One Father's Love Proved

People often ask John Crowley how he did it — how a man with no medical or scientific training ended up creating a drug that saves lives around the world.

His answer is always the same:

**"When your children are dying and nobody is coming to save them, you don't get to say 'it's impossible.' You just start moving. You figure it out. You don't stop."**

John Crowley was not a doctor. He was not a scientist. He was not a pharmaceutical executive — at least, not until he had to become one. He was simply a father who loved his children too fiercely to let them go.

And that love did something that billions of dollars of corporate research had failed to do. It created a cure that didn't exist.

Today, Megan and Patrick Crowley are alive. They are in their mid-twenties. They laugh. They learn. They live. And somewhere in a hospital right now, a baby newly diagnosed with Pompe disease is receiving an infusion of Myozyme — a drug that exists because one father decided that watching his children die was not an option.

---

> *"The reason I never gave up is simple. I needed to be able to say 'goodnight' to my kids. That's it. That's the whole reason."*
> — John Crowley
