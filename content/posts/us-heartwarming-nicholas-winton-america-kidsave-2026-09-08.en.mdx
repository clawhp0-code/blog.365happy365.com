# "A Stranger's Liver Saved My Daughter" — Meghan Speranzo and Baby Lora's Story

## A Race Against Time

In 2014, Heather and Jacob Lange of Wisconsin received the most devastating diagnosis any parent can hear. Their infant daughter, Lora, just a few months old, was diagnosed with **biliary atresia** — a rare and life-threatening liver disease in which the bile ducts inside or outside the liver are abnormally blocked. Without intervention, the condition leads to rapid liver failure and death, typically before the age of two.

Doctors were unequivocal: Lora needed a liver transplant, and she needed it soon. But finding a suitable organ for an infant is exceptionally difficult. A full adult liver is far too large for a baby's tiny body. The only viable option was a **living donor transplant** — someone willing to undergo major surgery to have a portion of their healthy liver removed and transplanted into the child.

The family was tested. No one matched. Friends stepped forward. None were compatible. The clock was ticking, and Lora's skin grew more jaundiced by the day.

## A Facebook Post That Changed Everything

Desperate and running out of options, Heather did what so many modern parents do in moments of crisis — she turned to social media. She posted Lora's photo along with a heartfelt plea explaining her daughter's condition and the urgent need for a living liver donor with a compatible blood type. The post spread through Wisconsin communities and beyond.

Roughly 250 miles away, **Meghan Speranzo**, a 22-year-old who had just graduated from college, scrolled past the post. She stopped. She looked at the photo of the tiny baby with tubes and wires, and something shifted inside her.

"I had never met this child," Meghan later recalled. "But when I saw those little eyes, I just knew — if I could help, I had to."

Meghan contacted the hospital immediately. What followed was weeks of rigorous medical testing: blood work, imaging, psychological evaluations, and detailed consultations about the risks of living donor surgery. Surgeons explained clearly that the procedure would involve a significant abdominal incision, weeks of painful recovery, months before full return to normal activity, and a small but real risk of serious complications — including death.

Meghan never wavered. Not once.

## The Surgery

In the summer of 2014, at a children's hospital in Wisconsin, the surgery commenced. Meghan was wheeled into the operating room first. Surgeons carefully removed the **left lateral segment** of her liver — a portion small enough to fit inside an infant's abdomen, yet large enough to sustain life. In a second operating room, the transplant team prepared baby Lora.

The surgery lasted over ten hours. Outside, two families who had been strangers just weeks before sat side by side, praying together, holding hands, waiting.

The surgery was a success.

Within days, Lora's new liver began functioning. The jaundice that had turned her skin yellow faded. Her appetite returned. Her eyes brightened. For the first time in her short life, Lora Lange had a future.

When Heather visited Meghan in the recovery room, she could barely speak through her tears.

"You saved my daughter," she whispered, gripping Meghan's hand. "How do I ever thank you for that?"

Meghan smiled through her own pain and exhaustion.

"Watching Lora grow up healthy — that's all the thanks I'll ever need."

## From Strangers to Family

What happened after the surgery surprised everyone, perhaps even Meghan and the Langes themselves. The two families, connected by an act of extraordinary generosity, became inseparable. Meghan visited on Lora's birthdays. She was there for first steps and first words. The Langes began calling Meghan their **"angel"** — not as a metaphor, but as a simple description of what they believed her to be.

As Lora grew older and began to understand the story of her own survival, she would run to Meghan with open arms whenever she saw her. The bond between donor and recipient transcended biology. It became something deeper — a chosen family, forged not by blood but by sacrifice.

In interviews, Meghan reflected on the experience with characteristic humility:

"I went into this thinking I was giving something to save a child. But Lora saved me too. She showed me what life is really about — that the most meaningful thing you can do is give a part of yourself so someone else can live."

## A Ripple of Inspiration

Meghan Speranzo's story resonated far beyond the two families it directly touched. Local and national media covered the story, sparking conversations about living organ donation across the country. The hospital reported a noticeable increase in living donor inquiries in the months following the surgery.

The numbers underscore why stories like Meghan's matter. According to the **Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN)**, more than 100,000 Americans are on the organ transplant waiting list at any given time. Every day, approximately 17 people die waiting for an organ that never comes. Living donation — particularly for kidneys and livers — remains one of the most effective ways to close that gap, yet it is still relatively rare because so few people know it is possible or are willing to accept the risks.

Meghan was not a doctor. She was not a soldier. She had no training in heroism. She was a 22-year-old, freshly graduated, standing at the beginning of her adult life, who saw a stranger's baby in need and decided that the risk to herself was worth the chance to save a life.

## The Quiet Courage of "I Can, So I Must"

There is a particular kind of bravery that does not announce itself. It does not arrive on a battlefield or in a burning building. It shows up in a hospital waiting room, in the quiet decision to lie down on a surgical table and let doctors open your body so that a piece of you can live inside a child you have never met.

Meghan Speranzo embodied that quiet courage. Her motivation was disarmingly simple: **"If I can help, I have to."**

That single conviction — that ability implies responsibility — gave an eight-month-old baby the gift of a lifetime. Lora Lange is growing up healthy today. She runs, she laughs, she goes to school. Inside her, a piece of a stranger's liver grows alongside her, sustaining every breath, every heartbeat, every dream she will ever have.

And somewhere, Meghan Speranzo carries a scar across her abdomen — a permanent reminder that the most profound acts of love are often performed not for the people we know, but for the ones we choose to care about anyway.
