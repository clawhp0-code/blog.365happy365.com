## A Barefoot Man on a Freezing Sidewalk

On the night of November 14, 2012, temperatures in midtown Manhattan had plunged below freezing. Winds whipped through the concrete canyons around Times Square, and holiday shoppers hurried past with their collars turned up. Amid the rush, a man sat on the cold sidewalk near 42nd Street — barefoot. His feet were swollen, blistered, and red from the cold. Hundreds of people walked past him. Not one stopped.

## A 25-Year-Old Rookie's Decision

**Officer Lawrence "Larry" DePrimo**, a 25-year-old NYPD patrolman from Holmdel, New Jersey, was working a counterterrorism detail in Times Square that night. He was relatively new to the force, still carrying the wide-eyed earnestness of someone who became a cop to help people.

When he spotted the barefoot man, he walked over and asked if he was okay. The man didn't say much. Larry looked down at those ravaged feet and made a decision that would take all of sixty seconds — but would echo for years.

He walked into a nearby Skechers shoe store on 42nd Street, pulled $75 from his own wallet, and bought a pair of all-weather insulated boots and a pair of thermal socks. The store employee gave him a police discount, but it was still a meaningful sum for a young officer's salary.

Larry returned to the man, knelt down on the cold pavement, and **personally slid the socks and boots onto the stranger's swollen feet**. No sermon. No conditions. No expectation of anything in return. Just one human being caring for another.

## The Photograph Heard Around the World

What Larry didn't know was that someone was watching. **Jennifer Foster**, a communications director for the Pinal County Sheriff's Office in Florence, Arizona, was visiting New York as a tourist. She saw the entire scene unfold and instinctively pulled out her phone to capture it.

The photograph shows Larry in full NYPD uniform, kneeling on the sidewalk, gently fitting the boots onto the man's feet. The image radiates a warmth that seems impossible on such a cold night.

Jennifer posted the photo to the NYPD's official Facebook page with a short note:

> "I saw the most beautiful thing tonight. A police officer knelt down and put boots on a barefoot homeless man in Times Square."

Within hours, the post went viral. It eventually garnered **millions of likes and shares**, becoming one of the fastest-spreading social media posts of 2012. CNN, ABC News, the BBC, The New York Times, and media outlets across the globe picked up the story. In a world that often seemed saturated with cynicism, Larry DePrimo's simple act cut through like sunlight.

## "I Didn't Do Anything Special"

Overnight, Larry became one of the most talked-about people in America. But in every interview, he deflected the praise with quiet sincerity.

> "I just did what I felt I had to do. When I saw that man's feet — blistered, cracked, bleeding in the cold — I just couldn't walk away. Nobody should have to be barefoot on a night like that."

He revealed that he had kept the receipt from the boots tucked inside his body armor vest. When a reporter asked why, his answer became almost as famous as the photograph itself:

> "On a hard day, I want to remember why this job is worth it."

## An Imperfect Ending — and a Deeper Lesson

Journalists later tracked down the man who received the boots. His name was **Jeffrey Hillman**, and his situation turned out to be more complex than the narrative suggested. He wasn't entirely without resources — he had a subsidized apartment and had experienced intermittent homelessness. Days after the story broke, he was photographed barefoot again. Jeffrey told reporters he had hidden the boots because they were "too nice" and he feared they would be stolen.

Some people were disillusioned. But Larry DePrimo offered a response that carried even more wisdom than the original act:

> "Whatever happened to those boots, the fact remains that in that moment, that man's feet were warm. I can't control outcomes. I can only control the choice I make in the moment."

That statement reframed the entire conversation. The value of kindness doesn't depend on a perfect Hollywood ending. It lives in the **decision to act** — in the willingness to kneel on a freezing sidewalk when everyone else keeps walking.

## Larry's Life After the Spotlight

DePrimo continued to serve with the NYPD long after the media attention faded. He patrolled the same streets with the same quiet dedication. The department officially commended him, and his story was incorporated into NYPD academy training as an example of compassionate policing. He never sought to monetize his fame and repeatedly emphasized that countless officers perform similar acts of kindness every day without anyone noticing.

## Why This Story Still Matters

In a city of 8 million people, on a street where hundreds walked past a suffering man without a second glance, one person stopped. He didn't have to. It wasn't in his job description. No supervisor ordered him to do it. He simply **chose** to see another human being.

We live in an era that often asks what grand, sweeping gestures can fix a broken world. Larry DePrimo's story suggests the answer might be far simpler: **a pair of socks, a pair of boots, and the willingness to kneel.**

Sometimes the most powerful thing you can do is stop walking, look down, and say, *"Are you okay?"*

> *"On a hard day, I want to remember why this job is worth it."*
> — Officer Larry DePrimo, NYPD
