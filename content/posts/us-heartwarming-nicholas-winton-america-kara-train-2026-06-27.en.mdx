## "Just Give Me Five Minutes" — Kevin Briggs, the Golden Gate Bridge Guardian Who Talked Hundreds Back from the Edge

### The Most Beautiful — and Deadliest — Bridge in the World

San Francisco's Golden Gate Bridge is an icon of American engineering and beauty, drawing more than ten million visitors every year. But since its opening in 1937, it has also held a grim distinction: it is one of the most common sites for suicide attempts anywhere on Earth. A fall from its 220-foot deck into the frigid Pacific waters below is survivable only about two percent of the time.

For most people, this is a troubling statistic they read and forget. For **Kevin Briggs**, a sergeant with the California Highway Patrol (CHP), it was his daily reality.

---

### The Reluctant Guardian

Kevin Briggs was assigned to the Golden Gate Bridge beat in 1994. His official duties included traffic enforcement and accident response, but it quickly became clear that his most critical role would be something no training manual had fully prepared him for: crisis intervention on the bridge's pedestrian walkway.

The first time Briggs encountered someone standing on the wrong side of the railing, he was paralyzed with fear. He had no script, no specialized psychology degree, no magic words. So he did the only thing that felt human.

He introduced himself.

"Hi. My name's Kevin. It looks like you're having a tough day. Can you give me a few minutes?"

What Briggs discovered over the following years was profound in its simplicity. The vast majority of people standing on that railing did not truly want to die. **They wanted the pain to stop.** And in that desperate moment, if someone — anyone — showed genuine interest in their story, many of them could be persuaded to step back.

Briggs did not rush. He did not lecture. He did not judge. Sometimes he stood beside a person for an hour, sometimes for two, sometimes longer. He listened. He asked questions. He acknowledged pain without minimizing it. And then, gently, he offered his hand.

---

### Kevin Hines: "The Moment I Let Go, I Wanted to Live"

One story that profoundly shaped Briggs's mission was that of **Kevin Hines**, a 19-year-old college student suffering from bipolar disorder who went to the bridge on September 25, 2000. Hines later recounted that he cried on the bus ride there, hoping someone — anyone — would ask if he was okay. No one did. He stood on the bridge for 40 minutes, weeping. A tourist approached him, but only to ask him to take a photograph.

Hines jumped.

During the four-second, 220-foot fall, a single thought consumed him: **"The moment my hands left the railing, I realized that every problem I thought was unfixable was actually fixable — except for what I had just done."**

Miraculously, Hines survived. He shattered vertebrae and nearly drowned, but a sea lion — witnesses later confirmed — appeared to bump him upward, keeping him afloat until the Coast Guard arrived. Hines became one of fewer than 40 known survivors of a jump from the Golden Gate Bridge.

He went on to become a world-renowned suicide prevention advocate, and his story reinforced everything Briggs had come to believe: **everyone on that railing was waiting for someone to speak first.**

---

### More Than 200 Lives, One Conversation at a Time

Over his career, Kevin Briggs is credited with talking **more than 200 people** off the Golden Gate Bridge. When asked for a precise number, he always deflected.

"The number doesn't matter. What matters is the one person standing in front of me right now."

Each encounter was different. Some people wanted to talk about lost jobs, broken marriages, terminal diagnoses, crushing debt. Others could barely articulate their pain. Briggs met each person where they were. He learned to read body language, to notice the subtle signs — the person standing alone, staring not at the view but at the water, gripping the rail with white knuckles.

He also learned the devastating weight of failure. Not everyone could be reached. Some people jumped before he could get close. Others refused to engage. Each loss carved itself into Briggs's memory. He carried those faces with him, and they exacted a toll.

---

### The Guardian Who Needed Guarding

The public saw a hero, but behind the uniform, Briggs was crumbling. Years of confronting death — and the accumulated grief of those he could not save — drove him into severe **post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)** and depression. He has spoken openly about reaching a point where he himself contemplated ending his life.

What pulled him back was the very thing he had given to others: connection. His family, his colleagues, and — perhaps most powerfully — the letters and messages from people he had saved became his lifeline. Former bridge visitors wrote to tell him they had gotten married, had children, found new careers, rediscovered joy. One woman told him her daughter, born years after that day on the bridge, was named **Hope**.

---

### A Second Life in Service

Briggs retired from the CHP in 2013 and launched a new career as a public speaker and mental health advocate. That same year, he delivered a **TED Talk** that has been viewed millions of times, bringing global attention to the crisis on the bridge and the broader epidemic of suicide.

In 2022, he published his memoir, **"Guardian of the Golden Gate,"** a raw and unflinching account of the lives he touched, the lives he lost, and his own battle with darkness. The book's foreword was written by Kevin Hines, the young man who jumped and survived, now a close friend and fellow advocate.

Briggs has testified before legislators, trained law enforcement agencies across the country, and helped build momentum for the installation of a steel net suicide deterrent system on the Golden Gate Bridge — a project decades in the making that finally began installation in recent years.

---

### "Anyone Can Do What I Did"

Kevin Briggs insists he is not extraordinary. In every interview, every lecture, every page of his book, he returns to the same message:

**"You don't need a badge to save a life. You need two words: 'You okay?' That's it. That's the whole secret."**

He urges ordinary people — coworkers, neighbors, family members, even strangers on a bus — to pay attention to the signs of suffering around them and to overcome the fear of saying the wrong thing.

"The wrong thing," he says, "is saying nothing."

For twenty years, on the most beautiful and most heartbreaking bridge in the world, Kevin Briggs stood in the space between despair and hope. He did not perform surgery. He did not administer medicine. He simply showed up, looked another human being in the eye, and said:

**"Tell me your story. I'm listening."**

And more than two hundred hearts kept beating because he did.
