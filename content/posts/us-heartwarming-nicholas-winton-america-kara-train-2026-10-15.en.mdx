## A Freezing Night in Times Square

On the evening of November 14, 2012, temperatures in Manhattan hovered near freezing. Twenty-five-year-old Larry DePrimo, a rookie officer with the New York City Police Department, was working a counterterrorism patrol near Times Square. It was a routine assignment — walk the blocks, stay visible, stay alert.

Then he saw something that stopped him in his tracks.

A man was sitting barefoot on the cold concrete at the corner of 42nd Street and 7th Avenue. No shoes. No socks. His feet were covered in blisters and raw, reddened skin. In the biting November wind, the homeless man sat quietly, asking no one for help.

## Seventy-Five Dollars and a Pair of Boots

Thousands of people had already walked past the man that night. Larry DePrimo was not one of them.

Without hesitation, he walked into a nearby Skechers shoe store. He pulled out his wallet — a rookie cop's wallet, modest at best — and paid $75 for a pair of all-weather boots and a set of warm thermal socks.

He returned to the man, knelt down on the frozen sidewalk, and carefully slid the socks onto the stranger's damaged feet. Then he laced up the new boots, one eyelet at a time, making sure they fit properly.

"Thank you," the man said softly. "They're warm."

Larry smiled, wished him well, and returned to his patrol. He told no one. He filed no report. To him, it was simply what you do when someone is suffering in front of you.

## A Photo Seen by Millions

But someone had been watching.

Jennifer Foster, a tourist visiting from Arizona, had witnessed the entire scene from across the street. She took out her phone and captured the image: a young police officer on his knees, gently putting boots on a barefoot stranger under the glow of Times Square lights.

Foster sent the photograph to the NYPD's official Facebook page with a message: "I witnessed the most beautiful act of kindness tonight."

On November 27, the NYPD posted the image. What followed was extraordinary.

Within 24 hours, the photo had hundreds of thousands of likes and shares. Within 48 hours, it was headline news across the globe — CNN, NBC, ABC, BBC, and countless international outlets all carried the story. The Facebook post ultimately received **more than 3.8 million likes**, making it one of the most-shared images in Facebook's history at that time.

## "I Didn't Do Anything Special"

When reporters tracked down DePrimo, the young officer seemed genuinely confused by the attention.

"I just did what I felt was right," he told NBC News. "When you see someone on a freezing night with no shoes, you do something about it. I think anyone would have done the same thing."

But the truth was, thousands had walked past. Only one person stopped.

His mother learned about her son's act of kindness from the news. Through tears, she told reporters: "I only taught him one thing — when you look at someone, put yourself in their shoes and think about what they're going through."

## The Ripple Effect

The photograph sparked a national conversation about homelessness, compassion, and the small gestures that define our humanity. Donations poured into homeless shelters across New York City. Shoe companies offered to donate thousands of pairs of boots. Strangers across the country began their own acts of kindness, sharing them online with the hashtag #DePrimo.

DePrimo was nominated for NYPD Officer of the Year and received commendations from city officials. But fame was never his goal. He quietly continued his work, patrolling the same streets, looking out for the same people most of us walk past without a second glance.

## What One Moment Can Teach Us

In a city of 8 million people, on a sidewalk passed by tens of thousands every hour, it took one 25-year-old rookie cop to remind the world of something we already knew but had forgotten: kindness doesn't require a platform, a budget, or an audience. It requires only the willingness to stop.

Larry DePrimo didn't kneel on that cold sidewalk for a photograph. He did it because a man's feet were freezing and he had the power to do something about it. That's all.

In a later interview, DePrimo offered a quiet reflection that captured the heart of the entire story:

> "I didn't give that man boots that night. He gave me something far greater — he reminded me why I became a police officer in the first place."

The photograph from that November night still circulates online every winter, a recurring reminder that the smallest act of human decency can echo across the world. It asks each of us one simple question: *When was the last time you stopped?*

In the end, Larry DePrimo's story isn't about boots or a viral photo. It's about the radical, quiet power of noticing another person — truly seeing them — and choosing to act. It's proof that in a world that often feels cold and indifferent, a single moment of warmth on a freezing sidewalk can change millions of hearts.

And sometimes, that's more than enough.
