# "There Were Kids Out There" — The Jabbar Gibson Story

## The Day New Orleans Drowned

On August 29, 2005, Hurricane Katrina made landfall along the Gulf Coast of the United States with sustained winds of 175 miles per hour. Within hours, the levees protecting New Orleans — a city largely built below sea level — catastrophically failed. Eighty percent of the city flooded. Tens of thousands of residents who had been unable or unwilling to evacuate found themselves trapped on rooftops, in attics, and on highway overpasses, waiting for help that was agonizingly slow to arrive.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) response became synonymous with bureaucratic failure. State and federal officials pointed fingers at one another while people died of dehydration, heat exposure, and drowning. The Superdome and the Ernest N. Morial Convention Center became overcrowded shelters of last resort, filled with desperate families and devoid of adequate food, water, or medical supplies.

Into this chaos stepped a young man whom nobody had asked to be a hero.

## Jabbar Gibson: An Unlikely Rescuer

Jabbar Gibson was a 20-year-old resident of New Orleans East, one of the neighborhoods hardest hit by the flooding. He was not a licensed bus driver. He had no emergency management training. In fact, he had a minor criminal record — the kind of background that, in the eyes of many, disqualified a person from the title of "hero."

But on that Monday morning, as the water rose to waist level in his neighborhood, Jabbar saw something that overrode every other consideration: **people who needed to get out, and a bus that could take them.**

He made his way to a flooded school bus depot, found a yellow school bus with keys still accessible, and turned the ignition.

## Into the Floodwaters

Jabbar drove through streets submerged under several feet of water. Downed power lines, floating debris, and abandoned vehicles turned every block into an obstacle course. He stopped at every cluster of stranded residents he could find — mothers holding infants above the waterline, elderly neighbors clinging to porch railings, teenagers wading through chest-deep water with nothing but the clothes on their backs.

He loaded them onto the bus. When the bus was full, he drove to higher ground, let people off to safety, and then turned back into the flood to find more.

After multiple runs through the drowning city, Jabbar had approximately **70 evacuees** on board — men, women, children, and elderly residents. Rather than wait for a government that had not yet arrived, he made a decision that stunned officials hundreds of miles away: **he drove the bus all the way to Houston, Texas.**

The journey was roughly 350 miles. It took about 13 hours through rain, traffic, and roads clogged with other fleeing residents. But Jabbar kept driving.

## First to Arrive

When the yellow school bus pulled up to the Houston Astrodome — which the city of Houston had opened as a mega-shelter for Katrina evacuees — it was the **first vehicle carrying New Orleans survivors to arrive.** Not a FEMA bus. Not a National Guard convoy. Not a Red Cross transport. A commandeered school bus driven by a 20-year-old with no official authorization.

Authorities at the Astrodome were bewildered. "Who sent you?" they asked. Nobody had sent him. "What agency are you with?" No agency. For a brief, surreal moment, there was talk of charging Jabbar with theft of the bus. But the survivors who stepped off that bus — dehydrated, exhausted, but alive — told a different story.

**"This man saved our lives,"** they said, one after another.

The charges were never filed. Instead, Jabbar Gibson became a national news story overnight.

## The Complicated Aftermath

As with many real-life heroes, the aftermath was not a clean, Hollywood ending. Some media outlets focused on Jabbar's prior brushes with the law rather than his extraordinary act of courage. The cultural narrative around Katrina was deeply entangled with race and class — the overwhelming majority of those stranded in New Orleans were Black and poor — and Jabbar's story became part of that complicated conversation.

In interviews, Jabbar was remarkably humble. **"I just saw people who needed help,"** he said. **"The bus was right there. I didn't think about it. I just did it."**

One mother who had been on the bus told reporters: *"I was on my roof for six hours with my two babies. Nobody came. Then this young man pulled up in a school bus and opened the door. I thought God sent him."*

## What Jabbar Gibson Taught America

Hurricane Katrina killed more than 1,800 people. It displaced over one million residents. It exposed systemic failures in disaster preparedness, emergency response, and the social safety net. Congressional hearings were held. Reports were written. Agencies were restructured.

But amid that colossal institutional failure, one truth shone through with blinding clarity: **when systems collapse, it is ordinary people who save lives.**

Jabbar Gibson did not have a title, a badge, or a mandate. He had a bus, a full tank of gas, and the refusal to stand by while his neighbors drowned. He drove through floodwaters that could have killed him. He made trip after trip when he could have left after the first. And he drove 350 miles through the night to deliver strangers to safety.

He never received a medal. No monument bears his name. But the children who rode that bus are now adults in their twenties, and they carry with them the memory of the morning the world was underwater and a yellow school bus came cutting through the flood.

## A Question for All of Us

As the 20th anniversary of Hurricane Katrina approaches in 2025, Jabbar Gibson's story asks us a question that transcends any single disaster:

*If the system fails, if no one gives you an order, if no one grants you permission — will you still move?*

Heroes are not defined by their résumés. They are defined by the moment they choose to act. Jabbar Gibson chose to act. And seventy people are alive because he did.
