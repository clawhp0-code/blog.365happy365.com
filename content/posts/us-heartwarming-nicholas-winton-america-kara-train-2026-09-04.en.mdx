## A Bridge of Beauty and Sorrow

The Golden Gate Bridge, with its Art Deco towers rising through San Francisco's fog, is one of the most photographed structures on Earth. But since its opening in 1937, it has carried a far darker distinction: it is the most-used suicide site in the United States. The railing stands only about four feet high. Below lies a 220-foot drop into the frigid waters of the Pacific.

For nearly two decades, one man stood between despair and that fall.

## The Quiet Guardian

**Kevin Briggs** joined the California Highway Patrol (CHP) in the 1990s. Assigned to the Marin County unit, his beat included regular patrols across the Golden Gate Bridge. Early in his career, he received a call about a man standing on the wrong side of the railing. Briggs had no specialized crisis training at the time. He simply walked up and started talking.

That first conversation changed the trajectory of his career — and, over the years, would save more than **292 lives**.

Briggs developed an approach that was disarmingly simple. He never shouted commands. He never rushed. He would walk slowly toward the person and ask one quiet question:

*"Can I take one step closer?"*

Then he would wait. Sometimes for minutes. Sometimes for hours. He asked their name. He asked about their family, their favorite food, what they had planned for the weekend. Ordinary questions — but on the edge of a bridge, ordinary questions are lifelines. They pull a person out of the abyss in their mind and back into the texture of living.

> "Most people don't really want to die," Briggs later explained. "They want the pain to stop. If you can show them that someone is willing to listen, that is often enough."

## March 11, 2005 — The Day He Met Kevin Berthia

On a fog-wrapped morning in 2005, Briggs spotted a young Black man standing on the exterior of the bridge railing, gripping the cold steel behind him. **Kevin Berthia** was 22 years old. He had recently become a father, but crippling depression, financial stress, and a sense of overwhelming inadequacy had driven him to this precipice.

Briggs approached without drawing his weapon or barking orders. He simply said:

*"What's your name?"*

Berthia didn't answer at first. The wind howled between the cables. Traffic roared past. But Briggs did not leave. For **92 minutes**, the sergeant stood beside the young man, leaning on the railing, talking in a calm, unhurried voice. He asked about the baby. He asked about Berthia's hopes, his fears, his anger. He acknowledged the pain without minimizing it.

At one point, Briggs said something that pierced through the fog in Berthia's mind:

*"The fact that you're still standing here tells me a part of you wants to live."*

After an hour and a half, Kevin Berthia climbed back over the railing and onto the bridge walkway. He collapsed into Briggs's arms and wept.

## Eight Years Later — A Reunion That Moved the World

Berthia received treatment, rebuilt his life, and went on to have more children. In **2013**, the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention held its annual gala. Berthia was invited — not as a guest, but as a presenter. He walked onto the stage and called Kevin Briggs forward.

Standing before hundreds of attendees, Berthia handed Briggs the **Bridge Rail Foundation Award** and said:

> "This man saved my life. But more than that, he showed me that my story was worth hearing."

The two men embraced. In the audience sat Berthia's wife and children — children who existed only because a patient officer had been willing to stand on a windy bridge for 92 minutes and listen.

A photograph of that embrace — the tall officer and the young father, clutching each other — went viral. It became one of the most shared images in the suicide-prevention community.

## After the Badge

Briggs retired from the CHP in **2013** at the rank of sergeant. But retirement did not mean silence. He launched a second career as a global speaker and mental health advocate. In **2014**, he delivered a **TED Talk** titled *"The Bridge Between Suicide and Life,"* which has since been viewed millions of times. In it, he shared lessons learned from two decades on the bridge:

- **Listen more than you speak.** A person in crisis needs to feel heard, not lectured.
- **Ask direct questions.** Asking someone if they are thinking about suicide does not plant the idea — it opens a door.
- **Stay present.** Sometimes the most powerful thing you can do is simply not walk away.

Briggs also wrote a book, *Guardian of the Golden Gate* (2015), chronicling his experiences and offering guidance for anyone who encounters a person in crisis.

## Kevin Berthia's New Mission

Berthia, too, transformed his near-tragedy into purpose. He became a vocal advocate for suicide prevention, speaking at schools, churches, and community centers. He works closely with the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention and shares his story to remind others that the darkness is not permanent.

> "I stand here today because one man took the time to ask my name," Berthia has said. "Now I spend my life asking other people theirs."

## 292 and Counting

Two hundred and ninety-two. To a statistician, it is a number. But behind that number are 292 mornings that someone woke up alive. 292 families that were not shattered. Children who were born. Graduations attended. Meals shared. Laughter that echoed through homes that might otherwise have gone silent.

Kevin Briggs carried no special weapon onto that bridge. He had no magic formula. He had only a willingness to walk one step closer, to speak one quiet sentence, and to stay — however long it took — until someone chose to live.

*"Can I take one more step closer?"*

It may be the most powerful question ever asked on the Golden Gate Bridge.
