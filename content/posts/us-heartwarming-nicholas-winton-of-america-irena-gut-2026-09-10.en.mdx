# "Go! Get Out! I've Got the Door!"
## The Holocaust Survivor Who Gave His Last Lecture Saving 22 Students

---

## A Boy Who Survived the Unthinkable

Liviu Librescu was born in 1930 in Ploiești, Romania, into a Jewish family. When World War II engulfed Europe, young Liviu was swept into the machinery of the Holocaust. He survived forced labor camps under Nazi occupation, enduring starvation, brutality, and the constant shadow of death. By the time the war ended, he had witnessed horrors that most people could never imagine.

After the war, Librescu pursued his passion for science, earning advanced degrees in aerospace engineering. But life under Romania's communist regime brought its own oppression. As a Jewish intellectual who refused to pledge loyalty to the Ceaușescu government, he was barred from publishing and marginalized professionally. With the help of Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin, who personally intervened with Romanian authorities, Librescu and his family emigrated to Israel in 1978.

In 1985, he accepted a faculty position at Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University — Virginia Tech — in Blacksburg, Virginia. There, in the quiet foothills of the Blue Ridge Mountains, the Holocaust survivor finally found the freedom he had sought his entire life. For more than 20 years, he taught solid mechanics and aeronautical engineering, publishing hundreds of papers and earning international recognition in his field. Students knew him as a demanding but deeply caring professor who always had time for anyone who knocked on his office door.

---

## An Ordinary Monday Morning

April 16, 2007, began like any other spring Monday on the Virginia Tech campus. Cherry blossoms were blooming, and students hurried across the Drillfield toward their morning classes. Professor Librescu, now 76 years old, walked into Room 204 of Norris Hall to teach his 9:05 a.m. solid mechanics class.

By a twist of fate — or perhaps something deeper — that day was **Yom HaShoah**, the annual Holocaust Remembrance Day observed in Israel and Jewish communities worldwide. The day set aside to remember the six million who perished was about to take on a devastatingly personal meaning.

At approximately 9:40 a.m., the sound of gunfire erupted inside Norris Hall. A lone gunman had chained the building's main doors shut and begun moving methodically from classroom to classroom, shooting students and faculty. Screams and the crack of bullets echoed through the corridors. In Room 204, panic seized the students as they realized what was happening.

---

## "Jump Out the Windows — Now!"

Professor Librescu did not freeze. He did not run. He turned to his students and shouted: **"Get out! Jump through the windows! Now!"**

Then the elderly professor pressed his body against the classroom door.

The gunman tried to force his way in. Librescu held the door with everything he had — his thin frame, his old hands, his entire will. Bullets tore through the wooden door and struck him, but he did not let go.

Behind him, one by one, his students pried open the second-floor windows and leaped to the ground below. One student. Then another. Then another. In those desperate seconds — the seconds that Librescu's body bought them — **22 of the 23 students in Room 204 escaped with their lives.**

When the gunman finally broke through, Librescu lay on the floor, mortally wounded. He died at the threshold of his own classroom.

---

## The Testimony of Survivors

In the days that followed the tragedy — the deadliest school shooting in modern American history, claiming 32 lives — the surviving students of Room 204 came forward to tell the world what their professor had done.

**Caroline Merrey**, one of the survivors, said: *"If it wasn't for him blocking the door, we would all be dead. He saved our lives."*

**Matt Webster**, another student, recalled: *"He was so brave. He blocked the door with his body. He knew he was going to die, but he did it so we could live."*

Student after student told the same story: the old professor shouting at them to go, his body against the door, the sound of bullets — and then the miracle of open air as they dropped from the windows to safety.

---

## The Weight of Remembrance

The cruel poetry of the date was lost on no one. On the very day the world paused to remember the victims of the Holocaust, a man who had survived that genocide gave his life to shield the young from another act of senseless violence.

Librescu's wife, **Marlena**, later reflected: *"He survived the Holocaust. He knew how precious life is. On that day, he did what he had to do."*

His son **Joe Librescu** said: *"My father would not have wanted to be called a hero. But he would have wanted everyone to know that life is worth protecting — no matter the cost."*

---

## A Legacy Carved in Stone and Spirit

Liviu Librescu's body was flown to Israel, where he was buried with full honors. The Israeli government and Romanian government both paid tribute to him. U.S. President George W. Bush personally honored his sacrifice. Romania posthumously awarded him its highest civilian decoration, the Order of the Star of Romania.

At Virginia Tech, 32 memorial stones — one for each victim — stand in a semicircle on the Drillfield. Librescu's name is etched among them, a permanent part of the campus he loved. The university established the **Liviu Librescu Memorial Scholarship** to support graduate students in engineering.

But his greatest legacy lives in the 22 people who walked away from Room 204 that morning — people who went on to graduate, build careers, start families, and live full lives because a 76-year-old professor refused to step away from a door.

---

## The Last Lesson

Some have asked: Why would a 76-year-old man sacrifice himself when he could have been the first to escape?

Perhaps the answer lies in the arc of his entire life. Liviu Librescu survived the Holocaust because, at critical moments, someone chose courage over self-preservation — a stranger who shared bread, a guard who looked the other way, a neighbor who hid a child. He knew, in the marrow of his bones, that survival is often a gift given by another's bravery.

When his moment came — when history repeated its cruelest pattern and a killer walked the halls — he did not hesitate. He became the door. He became the time. He became the reason 22 families did not receive the worst phone call of their lives that day.

His last lecture had no textbook, no equations, no final exam. It was the simplest and most profound lesson a teacher can offer:

**"Your lives matter more than mine. Go. I will hold the door."**

That lesson will never end.
