# "Get Up, I'll Carry You There" — The Story of Meghan Vogel

## A Gold Medalist's Second Race

On June 2, 2012, the Jesse Owens Memorial Stadium in Columbus, Ohio, was electric with the excitement of the Ohio High School Athletic Association (OHSAA) State Track and Field Championship. Hundreds of young athletes from across the state had gathered, each carrying the weight of months — sometimes years — of training.

Among them was **Meghan Vogel**, a 17-year-old junior from West Liberty-Salem High School, a small school in Champaign County. That morning, Meghan had already achieved something remarkable: she won the **1,600-meter state championship**, crossing the finish line first and claiming the gold medal. She was on top of the world.

But her day wasn't over. Meghan was also entered in the 3,200-meter race that afternoon. Having poured everything into her championship-winning performance just hours earlier, her legs were heavy, her lungs burned, and the exhilaration of victory was being steadily replaced by exhaustion. As the 3,200-meter race unfolded, Meghan found herself falling further and further behind. By the final lap, she was running in last place.

## Twenty Meters from the Finish Line

Just ahead of Meghan, another runner was struggling. **Arden McMath**, a competitor from Arlington High School, was in the grips of severe exhaustion. Her body was shutting down. With roughly 20 meters to go — the finish line almost close enough to touch — Arden's legs gave out completely. She collapsed on the track.

The crowd gasped. Arden tried to get up, her arms shaking, her knees buckling. She couldn't stand. Every second that passed, other runners had long since finished. It was just Arden, crumpled on the hot rubber surface, and Meghan, approaching from behind.

By every conventional measure, the smart thing for Meghan to do was simply run past. She was already in last place. Passing the fallen runner would move her up one position. There was no rule against it. No one would have blamed her.

**Meghan stopped.**

## "We're Going to Finish This Together"

Without a moment's hesitation, Meghan bent down and pulled Arden's arm over her own shoulder. She wrapped her other arm around Arden's waist and began walking — slowly, unsteadily — toward the finish line. Two exhausted teenage girls, strangers from rival schools, staggering forward together under the blazing Ohio sun.

The stadium fell silent. Then, one by one, spectators began to rise. Applause started softly and built into a roar. By the time the two girls reached the final meters, the entire stadium was on its feet, many in tears.

And then Meghan did something that elevated the moment from touching to transcendent: **she pushed Arden across the finish line first**, ensuring that the fallen runner would be recorded ahead of her. Meghan Vogel, the 1,600-meter state champion, officially finished the 3,200-meter race in **dead last**.

## "It Was Just the Right Thing to Do"

Reporters swarmed Meghan after the race. Her answer to every question was disarmingly simple:

> **"Helping her across the finish line was a no-brainer. I couldn't just leave her there. That's not how my parents raised me. It's nothing special — it's what you're supposed to do."**

Under OHSAA rules, a runner who receives physical assistance from another competitor can be disqualified. Officials reviewed the situation but chose not to penalize either athlete. The spirit of the rule, they decided, had not been violated. Something far greater than a rulebook had governed the track that afternoon.

## A Photograph Heard Around the World

A single photograph captured the moment: Meghan, face contorted with effort, holding Arden upright, both girls drenched in sweat, the finish line just steps away. The image went viral within hours. ESPN, NBC, ABC, and dozens of international outlets ran the story. Within days, Meghan Vogel's name was recognized far beyond the borders of Ohio.

She was nominated for an **ESPY Award** for Best Sports Moment. She received the National Sportsmanship Award from the U.S. Track and Field and Cross Country Coaches Association. Letters and emails poured in from around the world — from parents who used the story to teach their children, from coaches who showed the photograph to their teams, from strangers who simply needed to believe that goodness still existed.

Arden McMath's father wrote Meghan a letter that read, in part: **"When my daughter was at her lowest moment, you were there. You are an angel to our family."**

## First and Last on the Same Day

What makes Meghan Vogel's story resonate so deeply is the juxtaposition. On the same day, in the same stadium, the same athlete experienced both **first place and last place** — and the world remembers the last place finish far more vividly than the gold medal.

The gold medal proved she was a great runner. The last-place finish proved she was a great human being.

Meghan went on to run cross country at Cedarville University, but she never tried to capitalize on her fame. In interviews over the years, she has consistently deflected praise, pointing instead to her faith, her family, and the simple values she was raised with.

> **"A finish line is measured in seconds, but the real victory is defined by who you are when you get there,"** she once said.

## A Legacy Beyond the Track

Meghan Vogel's 30-second act on a June afternoon in 2012 has been shared millions of times. It has been used in classrooms, sermons, coaching clinics, and corporate leadership seminars as the definitive example of selflessness in competition. It reminds us that sports, at their best, are not about beating other people — they are about revealing who we truly are when the moment demands it.

Arden McMath recovered fully and continued her athletic career. The two young women, once strangers on a track, were connected forever by a moment that neither planned and neither will ever forget.

On that sweltering Ohio afternoon, a 17-year-old girl proved that you don't have to win the race to win the day. Sometimes, the most heroic thing an athlete can do is slow down, reach out a hand, and say: **"Get up. I'll carry you there."**
